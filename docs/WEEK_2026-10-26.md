## Honest frame
No one can promise 1M views per Short; the Action Lab says about 1% of its videos pass 1M. What we can do is pick topics
with proven demand, give every Short the shape the current 1M+ hits share, and keep every fact true. This plan does that.

**Recommendation (AK's call):** keep the seven-beat story, but cut these Shorts to **about 60 s** (~150 words): one
problem, one chase, one reveal, one twist. The full ~100 s format can stay for bigger stories (radio, fridge).

### 006 · Why do bottle caps have 21 teeth? (Mon 26 Oct)
**Script (~60 s)**
- **Hook:** Count the teeth on a bottle cap. Twenty-one. And this tiny cap led to the throw-away razor.
- **Problem:** Long ago, fizzy drinks were sealed with corks, wires or rubber stoppers. They leaked, and the fizz escaped.
- **Chase:** In 1892, William Painter patented a small metal cap with a crinkled edge. It was so cheap, his patent says,
  you could throw it away after one use. But it had to grip a glass bottle without cracking it.
- **Reveal:** A machine presses the cap down. The crinkles fold under the bottle's lip, and a soft liner squeezes tight
  against the glass. The teeth share the squeeze evenly, like many fingers holding a ball instead of one. Today the
  standard is twenty-one teeth.
- **Twist:** Painter's salesman was a man called King Gillette. Painter urged him to invent something like the cap: used
  once, then thrown away. Gillette came up with the throw-away razor blade.
- **Today:** One cap maker alone says it makes more than 32 billion bottle tops a year.
- **Callback:** Twenty-one teeth, one throw-away idea. What else do you use once and throw away?

**Visuals:** giant cap spinning, teeth counting up 1→21 with ticks; leaky cork bottle fizzing out; Painter's 1892 patent
page (US 468,226) slides onto the desk; cross-section: cap pressed by a machine head, teeth folding under the lip, pressure
arrows spreading evenly (fingers-on-ball analogy tagged); salesman with a cap tray turns into a razor with blades flying.
**Sources:** Painter patents US 468,226 and US 468,258 (2 Feb 1892; 468,258 text: "so inexpensive as to warrant
throwing them away after a single use"); ASME landmark brochure (stoppers "usually leaked or were not tight enough to hold
the carbonation gas"; Gillette "strongly influenced" by Painter); German Patent and Trade Mark Office poster on Painter
(21 teeth spread the pressure evenly); Crown Holdings patent filing US 2005/0029218 ("standardized on... 21 flutes");
Gillette patent US 775,134 (1904, blades "thrown away when dull"); Mansfield, *Invention & Technology* 1992 (Painter's
advice, paraphrased); Pelliconi (32 billion closures a year, one company).
**Don't say:** "the patent says 24 teeth" (only the Hall of Fame says 24); a word-for-word Painter quote; "always 21";
"pop came from the stopper" (the word dates from 1812); any world total of caps.
**Pinned comment:** "Count the teeth on the next cap you open. Did you get 21?"

### 007 · Why isn't your keyboard in ABC order? (Tue 27 Oct)
**Script (~60 s)**
- **Hook:** Your keyboard isn't in A-B-C order. But the very first typewriter keyboard was.
- **Problem:** In the 1860s, every letter and message was written by hand, slowly.
- **Chase:** In 1868, Christopher Sholes and his partners built a typing machine. Its first keys ran A to Z, like a
  piano. But its first big customers were telegraph operators, typing Morse code as fast as it beeped in. So the letters
  kept moving, year after year, to suit them. By 1882, the layout looked like yours.
- **Reveal:** You've probably heard it was made to slow typists down. The historians who traced it call that nonsense.
  Another idea is that it kept the metal typing arms from clashing. Experts still argue. Try this: the word
  "typewriter" fits on the top row.
- **Twist:** In 1936, a "scientific" layout was patented to beat it. It never took over. Nobody has clearly shown it's
  worth switching.
- **Callback:** From A-B-C, to Morse code, to the phone in your hand. Would you switch layouts if it were faster?

**Visuals:** a modern keyboard whose keys fly up and re-sort into A-Z piano keys; 1868 piano-style machine (patent US
79,265 drawing); telegraph sounder clicking dots and dashes as letters hop places; 1878 patent keyboard (US 207,559) morphs
into today's; "SLOW TYPISTS?" stamped MYTH; fingers sliding along T-Y-P-E-W-R-I-T-E-R on the top row; Dvorak keyboard
appears, then fades.
**Sources:** US 79,265 (23 Jun 1868, "key-board similar to the key board of a piano"); Yasuoka & Yasuoka, "On the
Prehistory of QWERTY", Zinbun 42 (Kyoto University, 2011): first machine's keys "in alphabetical order", layout "developed
for Morse receivers", today's form in the 1882 Remington Standard No. 2, the slow-down story "nonsense"; US 207,559 (1878,
QWERTY-like); ASME landmark brochure (the typebar-clash explanation); Dvorak & Dealey patent US 2,040,248 (1936);
Liebowitz & Margolis, "The Fable of the Keys", J. Law & Economics 1990 (no clear case for switching; contested).
**Don't say:** "designed to slow typists" as fact; "the 1868 patent shows QWERTY"; "QWERTY unchanged since 1873";
the "salesmen typed TYPEWRITER" story; "Dvorak is faster" or "isn't"; any count of keyboards in the world.
**Pinned comment:** "Try typing 'typewriter' using only the top row. What's the longest top-row word you can find?"

### 008 · Your phone needs Einstein to find you (Wed 28 Oct)
**Script (~60 s)**
- **Hook:** Satellites twenty thousand kilometres up have their clocks set wrong on purpose before launch, because
  Einstein said they'd run fast.
- **Problem:** For centuries, ships and travellers got lost. Knowing exactly where you are was a hard problem.
- **Chase:** In 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik. Two American scientists tracked it by how its radio signal
  changed pitch as it flew past. Then their boss flipped the idea: if you know where the satellite is, you can work out
  where you are. That became satellite navigation, and later GPS.
- **Reveal:** Each GPS satellite shouts the exact time. Your phone hears four of them and measures how late each signal
  arrives. That gives your distance from each, and where the distances meet, that's you.
- **Twist:** But high up, gravity is weaker, and Einstein showed weaker gravity makes clocks tick faster. So those clocks
  gain about 38 millionths of a second a day. Light travels about 11 kilometres in that time. Without the correction,
  GPS would drift that far off every day.
- **Today:** 32 GPS satellites, and nearly 6 billion navigation devices on Earth.
- **Callback:** Your phone uses Einstein every time you open a map. What would you do if GPS stopped for a day?

**Visuals:** satellite clock face with its hands nudged back by a hand before launch ("SET SLOW"); lost ship in fog;
Sputnik with a radio wave whose pitch slides (Doppler drawn as stretched/squeezed rings); four satellites throwing time
bubbles that meet at a phone dot; Earth's gravity well drawn as a funnel, clock ticking faster higher up; an 11 km map
drift with the dot sliding away; count-up 32 satellites.
**Sources:** NIST "Putting Einstein to the Test" (GPS clocks "run 38 microseconds per day faster", -7 special, +45
general); Ashby, "Relativistic Effects in the GPS" (AAPT 2006: factory frequency offset set before launch; errors build to
about 11.6 km a day); Ashby, Living Reviews in Relativity 2003 (four satellites because of the receiver clock bias);
GPS.gov (orbit about 20,200 km; one-way signals); Johns Hopkins APL Technical Digest (Guier and Weiffenbach tracked Sputnik
by Doppler; McClure "invert[ed] the problem", March 1958; Transit); NAVCEN constellation table (32 satellites, 25 Oct
2026); EUSPA (5.8 billion GNSS devices in use in 2024, all systems, not just GPS).
**Don't say:** "satellites are fast so their clocks run fast" (speed slows them; gravity wins); "your phone sends its
location to the satellite"; "your dot would be 10 km off" (say "would drift about 11 km a day"); "24 satellites";
"Sputnik's beeps"; "Guier and Weiffenbach invented Transit".
**Pinned comment:** "GPS clocks are set slow before launch so they come out right in orbit. Did you know your maps use
Einstein?"

### 009 · Why can't you pull a zipper apart? (Thu 29 Oct)
**Script (~60 s)**
- **Hook:** Pull the two sides of a closed zipper as hard as you like. They won't let go. Only one tiny part can open it.
- **Problem:** Shoes and boots were once done up with rows of hooks or buttons, one at a time.
- **Chase:** In 1893, Whitcomb Judson showed a "clasp locker" at the Chicago World's Fair. Almost nobody cared. It jammed.
  Then an engineer from Sweden, Gideon Sundback, spent years redesigning it. His new design used tiny matching cups that
  nest inside each other.
- **Reveal:** Each tooth has a bump on top and a hollow underneath. The slider has a Y-shaped channel. It brings the two
  rows together at an angle, so each bump drops into the next hollow. Once the teeth are in line, pulling sideways can't
  unhook them. Only the slider can tilt them apart.
- **Twist:** The name "zipper" didn't come from the inventor. A rubber-boot company picked it in the 1920s, from the
  sound.
- **Today:** Just one company, YKK, sold more than 9.7 billion zippers in a single year.
- **Callback:** How many zippers are you wearing right now?

**Visuals:** two hands tugging a zipper, strain lines, nothing moves; slider glides and it opens; a boot with a long row of
hooks; 1893 fair booth, crowd walking past, a "JAMMED" stamp; Sundback's patent drawing (US 1,219,881) slides in; macro
cut-away: cup-shaped teeth, the Y channel guiding them in at an angle, then locked in a straight line; "ZIP" sound word
from a rubber boot; counter to 9.7 billion.
**Sources:** Judson patents US 504,037 and 504,038 (29 Aug 1893; clasps engage only "at an angle to the line of strain");
Lemelson-MIT and Hagley (shown at the 1893 Chicago fair, ignored, "frequently jammed"); Sundback patent US 1,219,881
(filed 1914, granted 1917; cup-shaped members that nest; slider with "a Y-shaped channel"; "practically no movement of
separation" when engaged); National Inventors Hall of Fame (Sundback); YKK Americas and Hagley (the Goodrich company named
it after the "zip" sound, 1920s); YKK Group integrated report 2026 (9.73 billion zipper units, FY to March 2026).
**Don't say:** "Judson invented the modern zipper"; "Mr. B.F. Goodrich named it" (he died in 1888, the company did);
"designed in 1913" for the 1917 patent; a world market share; "Elias Howe invented it".
**Pinned comment:** "Try it on your jacket: pull the sides apart. Then explain to someone why it won't open."

### 010 · Claim Check: Japan's robot will grab dirt from a moon of Mars (Fri 30 Oct)
**Script (~60 s)**
- **Hook:** On Earth this little rover weighs about 25 kilograms. On the moon it's heading for, it will weigh about as
  much as a grape.
- **Problem:** Mars has two tiny moons, Phobos and Deimos. Nobody knows where they came from: a captured asteroid, or
  rubble from a giant crash into Mars.
- **Chase:** To find out, you need a piece of one. Robots have done this before, but only with asteroids. In 2010,
  Japan's Hayabusa brought back tiny grains. In 2020, Hayabusa2 brought back 5.4 grams. In 2023, NASA's OSIRIS-REx
  brought back about 120 grams. But nothing has ever been brought back from around Mars.
- **Reveal:** In October 2026, Japan launched MMX. It reaches Mars in 2027. Later it drops a small rover from about
  40 metres up, and grabs at least 10 grams of Phobos. Gravity there is about 1,700 times weaker than on Earth, so the
  rover falls slowly.
- **Twist:** The long part is the trip home. The sample is planned to land in Australia in 2031. [Stamp: LAUNCHED]
- **Callback:** A handful of dirt, five years, two worlds. If Phobos turns out to be a piece of Mars, what would you look
  for in it?

**Visuals:** rover on a kitchen scale reads 25 kg, then on a Phobos scale 15 g next to a grape; Mars with two potato-shaped
moons orbiting; split screen "captured asteroid?" vs "crash debris?"; relay of three capsules landing (2010 / 2020 /
2023) with their sample sizes as piles; H3 rocket launch; MMX orbiting, rover dropping slowly and bouncing; sample capsule
parachuting into an Australian desert; claim stamp LAUNCHED.
**Sources:** JAXA press release 20 Oct 2026 (H3 flight 10 launch, separation); JAXA MMX mission page ("a minimum of
10g", "Nothing has ever been returned from the Martian sphere before", 2031 return); DLR MMX facts and news (Idefix about
25 kg, lands on Phobos 2029, dropped from about 40 m); DLR/CNES Idefix launch kit (about 1/1,700 of Earth's gravity, "15
gram weight on Phobos – a grape!"); CNES MMX page (2031 return to the Australian desert; "Scientists are still debating
their origin"); ESA MMX factsheet (captured asteroid or impact debris); NASA Hayabusa page (return 13 Jun 2010); JAXA
Hayabusa2 page (5.4 g, Dec 2020); NASA OSIRIS-REx (121.6 g, 24 Sep 2023).
**Don't say:** "first sample from a moon" (our own Moon's came first; say "from around Mars"); "1/2000 gravity"; "lands in
2027"; the sample as if already returned; "the rover uses thrusters to land"; a big haul for Hayabusa 2010.
**Pinned comment:** "Captured asteroid or a piece of Mars? Place your bet before 2031."

### 011 · Changing the clocks was a bug collector's idea (Sat 31 Oct)
**Script (~60 s)**
- **Hook:** This Sunday, clocks across the USA and Canada go back an hour. Europe did it last weekend. The idea came from a
  bug collector.
- **Problem:** In summer the sun rises very early, while most people are still asleep. All that morning light goes unused.
- **Chase:** In 1895, George Hudson, a New Zealand insect collector, suggested moving clocks with the seasons. People
  laughed at him. In 1907, a British builder, William Willett, wrote a pamphlet called "The Waste of Daylight". He died
  before anyone listened. Then, in 1916, during the First World War, Germany moved its clocks, and Britain followed
  that May.
- **Reveal:** The trick is simple. The clock moves, not the sun. Put clocks forward in spring, and the sun seems to set
  later, so evenings stay light. In autumn we put them back.
- **Twist:** And Benjamin Franklin? His famous 1784 letter never mentions changing clocks. It was a joke about waking
  Paris with cannons at sunrise.
- **Callback:** All because a bug collector wanted more daylight. Would you keep the clock change, or end it?

**Visuals:** a wall clock spinning back an hour; sunrise over sleeping houses, light pouring into empty streets; a
butterfly net and specimen box (Hudson); Willett's pamphlet on the desk; 1916 newspaper-style clock; Earth turning with
the sun fixed while the clock hands move (the reveal); Franklin with a cannon going off at dawn, "JOKE" stamp.
**Sources:** NIST (US daylight saving time 8 Mar to 1 Nov 2026); 15 U.S.C. 260a ("first Sunday of November", 2 a.m.);
National Research Council Canada (first Sunday of November); European Commission (summertime ends the last Sunday of
October); Te Ara, Dictionary of New Zealand Biography, "Hudson, George Vernon" (entomologist; 1895 proposal; ridiculed);
Royal Museums Greenwich (Willett's 1907 "The Waste of Daylight"; died 1915); Hansard, Lords, 16 May 1916 (Summer Time Bill,
a wartime measure); Franklin, "An Economical Project", Journal de Paris, 26 Apr 1784 (cannons, candles, shutters; no clock
change; reprint at WebExhibits).
**Don't say:** "Franklin invented it"; "it was made for farmers"; "it gives us more daylight"; "Germany invented it"
(first nationwide, not first idea); Hudson's motive "to catch bugs" as fact (only a secondary source says so); a reason
for Germany's 1916 change (only secondary sources); the British Columbia change unless confirmed on gov.bc.ca.
**Pinned comment:** "Keep the clock change or end it for good? Tell us where you live."

### 012 · The barcode was drawn in beach sand (Sun 1 Nov)
**Script (~60 s)**
- **Hook:** In 1948 or 49, a man sitting on a beach pushed four fingers into the sand, pulled them towards him, and drew
  the first barcode.
- **Problem:** Supermarket checkouts were slow. Every price was typed in by hand.
- **Chase:** Norman Woodland knew Morse code. On that Miami beach, he stretched its dots and dashes into thin lines and
  thick lines. Then he swept them round into a circle, like a bull's-eye. He and Bernard Silver patented it in 1952. But
  the scanners didn't exist yet.
- **Reveal:** A barcode is a number written in stripes. A red laser sweeps across it. White stripes reflect light, black
  stripes don't, and the pattern of thick and thin becomes digits. The last digit is a check, so a wrong scan gets caught.
- **Twist:** The patent ran out in 1969, five years before the first barcode was ever scanned in a shop: a pack of gum,
  in Ohio, in 1974.
- **Today:** More than 10 billion barcode scans every day.
- **Callback:** All from four fingers in the sand. What was the last thing you bought with a barcode?

**Visuals:** fingers dragging through sand leaving four lines; a slow cashier typing prices; Morse dots and dashes
stretching into bars, then curling into a bull's-eye; Woodland and Silver's 1952 patent drawing (US 2,612,994) on the
desk; laser beam sweeping stripes with reflections and a digit counter; "PATENT EXPIRED 1969" stamp over a calendar
running to 1974; a gum pack beeping at a checkout; 10 billion counter.
**Sources:** Woodland, quoted in Smithsonian Magazine (Weightman, 2015): "I poked my four fingers into the sand... I swept
them round into a circle"; Drexel University obituary 2012 ("I just extended the dots and dashes downwards", winter 1948);
patent US 2,612,994 (filed 1949, granted 7 Oct 1952; straight lines and a circular version; Google Patents lists
expiration 7 Oct 1969); GS1 "How we got here" (26 Jun 1974, Marsh supermarket, Ohio, a pack of Wrigley's gum, first
product scanned) and GS1 About ("+10 billion barcode scans every day"); GS1 check-digit page; NMAH record for the 1974
Spectra-Physics scanner (laser + rotating mirror; the gum pack "never came to the Smithsonian").
**Don't say:** "the gum pack is in the Smithsonian"; "the bull's-eye is what's on products today" (the rectangular code
was a different design, chosen in 1973); "8:01 a.m." exactly; "Juicy Fruit 10-pack" as museum fact (only a state
historical marker says so); "1948" alone for the beach (say "1948 or 49").
**Pinned comment:** "Woodland got the idea from Morse code. What everyday thing would you turn into a code?"

## Visuals: what to improve (AK: "In Visuals you can improve")
1. **One giant hero object from the first frame,** filling most of the screen (the cap, the zipper, the keyboard), drawn
   with light and shadow so it reads as a real thing, not a diagram. The breakout Shorts all open on the object, big.
2. **Macro cut-aways for the mechanism:** zoom into the zipper's teeth, the cap's crimp, the barcode under the laser. The
   "inside view" is the payoff people rewatch.

4. **Faster scene rhythm in the first 3 seconds:** a change every ~1 s until the question lands, then settle.
5. **Real-world scale cues** (a grape next to the rover, a hand next to the cap) so sizes mean something.
