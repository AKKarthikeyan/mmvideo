# IC-01 script, draft 2: The Lakshman Rekha Isn't in Valmiki's Ramayana

Draft 2, 25 Oct 2026. It replaces draft 1 (`IC01_SCRIPT_v1.md`, kept for the record).

**What changed:** draft 1 told the viewer *what* Valmiki says differently. Draft 2 also tells them *why it matters to their own life*: one plain takeaway after each of the five scenes, marked OUR NOTE because it is our reading, not the text. And the ending now ties the five into one idea.

**Unchanged rules:**
- Every quote card is Ralph T. H. Griffith's exact wording (1870–74, public domain), pulled from `corpus.py`, with Griffith's book and canto.
- Rama, Sita and the epic's figures appear as backlit silhouettes in light (rule 5, extended 25 Oct).
- Not quoted: Lakshmana's lines on "womankind" and Sita's words about ending her life (both in 3.45).

**Voicing: in character (AK approved, 10 Nov 2026).** Each quote card is marked with who speaks it, and those lines get their own voice, heard once and never re-read. NARRATOR cards use the narrator's voice.

**Script locked, 10 Nov 2026**, apart from the reviewer's four checks below. No reviewer is named yet; the video can't be published until one signs.

## What this video is about, and what the viewer takes home

**In one line:** the Ramayana most of us know grew over centuries; this is what its oldest source says, and why the differences matter for how we live.

**Who it's for:** someone about to see *Ramayana: Part 1* (5 Nov). Also anyone who grew up with the TV serial, or their grandmother's telling, and has never seen the source.

**What the viewer gets:**
1. **Five facts they can check and share.** Each is shown on screen with its book and canto.
2. **Five usable life ideas, one per scene:**
   - People can come back from a fall, but it takes their work *and* our welcome.
   - Real strength is quiet.
   - Never promise before you hear the terms.
   - Love is a choice made with open eyes, not obedience.
   - You can't draw a line around someone you love; you can warn, pray and let them choose.
3. **A way to watch the film,** and any retelling: ask which version it chose, and what that version needed.
4. **No verdict on which version is "right".** Later tellings are honoured traditions; the video says what each was doing.

**The one idea that ties it together:** in the oldest telling, these are people making choices: to return, to keep a promise, to speak up, to let go. That is why a 2,500-year-old story still has something to say to us.

### 0:00 · Cold open (Mystery)
**Picture.** Night. A forest hut. A line of light glows in the dust before the door, and hums.
**Voice (narrator).**
> In the oldest Ramayana we have, where is the line Sita must not cross?
> You know the one. A line in the dust, outside a hut in the forest.
> Cross it, and you're on your own.
> Millions of us grew up with that line.

*(Bell. The line flickers.)*

### 0:12 · Stake and promise
**Picture.** The line fades. Title card: **THE INNER CHARIOT · VALMIKI, NOT THE FILM**.
> On the fifth of November, a film tells this story to millions.
> Before it does, let's open the source.
> I'll show you five places where the oldest telling says something different.
> You'll see the words on screen, with the book and the canto.
> And each one, it turns out, has something to say about how we live.
> By the end, you'll know what that line in the dust really was.

### 0:40 · Who Valmiki is
**Picture.** A riverbank at dusk. Two curlews. An arrow: one bird falls. A palm leaf, and on it the first lines writing themselves in.
> The story begins with a poet watching two birds.
> A hunter shoots one.
> Its mate cries out.
> And the poet, Valmiki, speaks a curse, in a rhythm no one had used before.

**Card (Griffith, Bala 1.2), voice: VALMIKI**
> "The measured form of words I spoke / In shock of grief be termed a śloke."

*(3 s silence.)*
> Grief, shaped with care, became the first verse.
> That verse grew into some twenty-four thousand, in seven books.
> Every quote you'll see is from the English of Ralph Griffith, made in the eighteen-seventies.

### 1:35 · One: Ahalya (Two Versions)
**Picture.** Split frame. Left, warm paper, "the version you know": a stone, a foot, light. Right, lamplight: a grove, a woman's silhouette, half there, like mist.
> You may know Ahalya as the woman turned to stone.
> Rama's foot touches the stone, and she's free.
> Turn the leaf.
*(The palm leaf turns; the frame goes to lamplight.)*
> In Valmiki, there's no stone.
> Her husband's curse is solitude.

**Card (Griffith, Bala 1.48), voice: GAUTAMA**
> "Thy bed the ashes, air thy food, / Here shalt thou live in solitude. … And not an eye thy form shall see."

*(3 s silence.)*
> She lives unseen, in penance, for years.
> And when the brothers find her, look who bows to whom.

**Card (Griffith, Bala 1.49), voice: NARRATOR**
> "Then, with great joy that dame to meet, / The sons of Raghu clapped her feet"

**Kicker: OUR NOTE**
> So it isn't a stone woken by a foot. It's a person, welcomed back.
> And that's harder than magic.
> Someone does the long work of changing.
> Someone else does the work of welcoming them home.
> If you've ever been the one who fell, or the one who has to forgive, you know both are needed.

### 3:20 · Two: the bow
**Picture.** Scale reveal: a great iron chest on eight wheels, hauled by a line of men that runs out of frame.
> But surely you know how Rama won Sita.
> A great assembly of kings. Each one tries the bow. Each one fails.
> In Valmiki, the kings had come before, and failed before.
> On this day, it's a quiet court. A sage. Two boys.
> And a bow so heavy it arrives on wheels.

**Card (Griffith, Bala 1.67), voice: NARRATOR**
> "Five thousand youths in number, all / Of manly strength and stature tall, / The ponderous eight-wheeled chest that held / The heavenly bow, with toil propelled."

*(3 s silence.)*
> Rama lifts it, "as if in sport," and draws the string, until the bow breaks in two.
> The whole court faints. Only the king, the sage and the two princes stay standing.

**Kicker: OUR NOTE**
> No crowd to beat. No show.
> In the oldest telling, real strength is quiet.
> It's simply doing the thing nobody else could lift.

### 4:40 · Three: the oath
**Picture.** Kaikeyi's chamber, lamplit. The king's silhouette, kneeling. A ring of light closes around his words.
> But the happiness doesn't last.
> So why does a king who loves his son send him away?
> Later tellings soften it. In some, the gods themselves turn Manthara's mind **(check)**.
> Valmiki keeps it human. Fear in one room, a careless promise in another.
> Kaikeyi asks the king to swear first, and only then to hear.
> And he does.

**Card (Griffith, Ayodhya 2.11), voice: DASHARATHA**
> "I swear by Ráma my dear son, / Speak, and thy bidding shall be done."

*(3 s silence.)*
> He swears by the very son the wish will send away.

**Kicker: OUR NOTE**
> This one's for all of us.
> Never promise before you hear the terms.
> A king lost his son to a yes he gave too early.

### 5:45 · Four: Sita insists
**Picture.** A doorway, and a road leading out of it. Close on two hands. Then Sita's silhouette steps into the light.
> So Rama must go. But he tells Sita to stay home, safe.
> You might expect her to agree quietly.
> She doesn't.

**Card (Griffith, Ayodhya 2.27), voice: SITA**
> "What words are these that thou hast said? / … I dismiss / With bitter scorn a speech like this"

*(2 s silence.)*
> And then the line she wins with:

**Card (Griffith, Ayodhya 2.27), voice: SITA**
> "My foot before thine own shall pass / Through tangled thorn and matted grass."

**Kicker: OUR NOTE**
> Valmiki's Sita doesn't follow Rama into the forest. She chooses it.
> Love, here, isn't obedience.
> It's a decision you make with your eyes open, and say out loud.

### 6:50 · The turn
**Picture.** Back to the hut. The line of light tries to draw itself. It stalls.
> So, the line in the dust.
> If you grew up with the TV serial, so did we.
> But here is the scene in Valmiki.
*(Bell.)*

### 7:05 · Five: the deer, and the missing line
**Picture.** A golden deer between the trees. Close on its eye. Lakshmana's silhouette, bow lowered, half turned.
> A golden deer appears near the hut.
> Sita wants it. Rama goes after it.
> And it's Lakshmana, not Rama, who sees through it.

**Card (Griffith, Aranya 3.43), voice: LAKSHMANA**
> "'Tis magic, for the world has ne'er, / Lord of the world, shown aught so fair."

*(3 s silence.)*
> Far away, a voice cries out in Rama's voice.
> It's a trick. Sita is terrified.
> She begs Lakshmana to go.
> He refuses. He was told to guard her.
> And in her fear, she accuses him of the worst motives a man could have.
> It's the hardest moment in the scene: fear, speaking as anger.
*(The flame dims. Deepest night.)*
> So Lakshmana goes.
> But listen to how he leaves.

**Card (Griffith, Aranya 3.45), voice: LAKSHMANA**
> "May all attendant wood-gods screen / Thy head from harm, O large-eyed Queen!"

*(3 s silence.)*

**Card (Griffith, Aranya 3.45), voice: NARRATOR**
> "His reverent hands once more he raised, / His head he slightly bent, / Upon her face he sadly gazed, / And then toward Ráma went."

> No line.
> No circle in the dust.
> His protection was a prayer.

**Kicker: OUR NOTE**
> And maybe that's the truest thing in the story.
> You can't draw a line around someone you love.
> You can warn them. You can pray for them.
> And then you have to let them choose.

### 8:50 · What each version needed (no verdict)
**Picture.** Three lamps on a ledge. Each lights in turn as it's named.
> So where does the line come from?
> It appears in later retellings. Scholars disagree about where it first appears.

**Kicker: OUR NOTE**
> Later tellers gave Sita a boundary drawn by someone who loved her: simple, sacred, safe.
> Valmiki gives her something harder: a warning, a prayer, and a choice.
> Neither version is wrong to tell. They're answering different questions.

### 9:30 · The thread, and the cinema
**Picture.** The five scenes as five small icons around the line of light, now steady and gold.
> Five scenes. One thread.
> In the oldest telling, these are people making choices.
> To come back. To keep a promise. To speak up. To let go.
> That's why a story this old still has something to say to us.
> So when you watch the film this week, look for these five moments.
> Ahalya. The bow. The oath. Sita's answer. And last, the line.
> Notice which version the film chooses, and ask what that version needed.
> And what would prove us wrong? A manuscript of Valmiki with the line in it.
> We don't know of one. If you do, tell us, with the source.

### 10:25 · Close
**Picture.** The hut at dawn. The doorway is open. End card.
> Next time: a woman alone in a grove of Ashoka trees, a demon king at the gate,
> and a single blade of grass between them.
> This was The Inner Chariot.
> Every verse here was shown with its book and canto. Our reading was marked as our note.
> The pictures are drawn in code, the voices are synthetic, and nobody here speaks for God.
