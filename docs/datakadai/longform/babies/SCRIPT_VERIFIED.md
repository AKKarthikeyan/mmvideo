## Cold open (0:00 to 0:30)

> In NFHS-4, the 2015-16 survey, Bihar's fertility rate was 3.4 children per woman. [C01]
> In NFHS-6, the 2023-24 survey, it is 2.7. [C02]
> Bihar is still the highest of all the places surveyed. [C20]
> In 30 of the 35 states and union territories in NFHS-6, the rate is at or below 2.1, a number demographers call the replacement level. [C03] [C18] [C08]
> So what is really happening to India's fertility rate? The answer depends on where you live, and the map holds some surprises. [C04]

**ON SCREEN:** Bihar 3.4 (NFHS-4, 2015-16) to 2.7 (NFHS-6, 2023-24); the 2.1 line; "NFHS-6 fact sheets: provisional".

## Act 1 · The magic number: 2.1 (0:30 to 2:00)

> Demographers watch one number above all: the total fertility rate, or TFR. [C07]
> It is the average number of children a woman would have by the end of her childbearing years, if she had children at the rates measured in the years just before the survey. [C07]
> So it is a snapshot of the recent past. It is not a count of how many children each family actually has. [C07]
> The magic line is 2.1. [C08]
> The United Nations says the replacement level is about 2.1 children per woman: enough, on average, for a woman to be replaced by a daughter who survives to childbearing age. [C08] [C09]
> Stay below that line for long enough, and if nobody moves in from elsewhere, a country's population eventually starts to shrink. [C10]
> One survey below 2.1 does not mean a population is shrinking. The key words are "long enough". [C10]
> Every figure for India and its states comes from the National Family Health Survey, conducted by the health ministry with the International Institute for Population Sciences. [C62]
> The sixth round, NFHS-6, covered nearly 6.8 lakh households. We use its fact sheets, which are provisional. [C55] [C56]
> The counts and comparisons are our own arithmetic. We will say so when we go further. [C31]

**ON SCREEN:** the 2.1 line drawn across the screen; one woman icon and one daughter icon with the words "a daughter who survives to childbearing age (UN)"; chip "Source: IIPS, NFHS-6 fact sheets, May 2026 (provisional)"; chip "UN DESA" on the replacement-level sentence.

## Act 2 · India crosses the line (2:00 to 3:30)

> Let us follow India's own line. [F] In the first round, in 1992-93, it was 3.4. [C63]
> In NFHS-3, in 2005-06, it was 2.7. [C15]
> In NFHS-4, in 2015-16, it was 2.2, just above the line. [C11]
> In NFHS-5, in 2019-21, it fell to 2.0. [C12] That was the first NFHS round to put India below 2.1. [C13]
> NFHS-6, whose results the health ministry released on 29 May 2026, shows 2.0 again. [C14] [C17] India's rate stays below the line. [C14]
> But India is not one number. It has 36 states and union territories, and the NFHS-6 release covers 35 of them. [C16]
> Manipur is not in the NFHS-6 release. [C22]
> Those 35 places live in very different demographic worlds. [C04]

**ON SCREEN:** line chart NFHS-1 3.4, NFHS-3 2.7, NFHS-4 2.2, NFHS-5 2.0, NFHS-6 2.0 with the 2.1 line crossing between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 (NFHS-1 and NFHS-3 from the NFHS-3 report; NFHS-2 not shown).

## Act 3 · The map: two Indias (3:30 to 5:30)

> Here is NFHS-6, state by state. [F]
> Of the 35 places, 30 are at or below 2.1. [C18] Twenty-eight are below it, and two, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan, sit right on the line at 2.1. [C18] [C21]
> At the bottom, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands read 0.9, the only place in the survey below one child per woman. [C19] Sikkim comes next, at 1.0. [C19]
> A caution on the islands: their figure rests on about 2,400 women, a small sample. [C57]
> At the top, Bihar reads 2.7. [C20] Four places follow at 2.2: Jharkhand, Meghalaya, Uttar Pradesh, and the island territory of Lakshadweep. [C20]
> Only five places in the survey are above 2.1. [C24]
> Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Telangana in the south, and Gujarat, Maharashtra and Goa in the west, all read 1.9 or lower. [C23]
> Between the lowest, 0.9, and the highest, 2.7, the gap is 1.8 children per woman. [C04]

**ON SCREEN:** the 16:9 India map filled by NFHS-6 TFR, top and bottom lists; "above replacement" and "below replacement" labels; Manipur drawn blank with "not in NFHS-6 release"; small tag "small sample" on Andaman and Nicobar Islands; the eight named south and west states outlined with their values (AP 1.8, Karnataka 1.8, Kerala 1.8, Tamil Nadu 1.7, Telangana 1.9, Gujarat 1.9, Maharashtra 1.8, Goa 1.6).

## Act 4 · The biggest falls (5:30 to 7:00)

> Meghalaya fell from 2.9 to 2.2, the biggest fall in the survey. [C25]
> Back in NFHS-4 it read 3.0, so most of the fall since then came between NFHS-5 and NFHS-6. [C29]
> The Andaman and Nicobar Islands fell from 1.3 to 0.9, again a small-sample place. [C26]
> Bihar, still the highest, fell from 3.0 to 2.7. [C27]
> Arunachal Pradesh, Assam and Mizoram each fell by 0.3 on the printed figures. Those are rounded to one decimal, so read that as about 0.3. [C28]
> Bihar went from 3.4, to 3.0, to 2.7. [C01] [C27]
> Assam went from 2.2, to 1.9, to 1.6. [C29] [C28]
> Meghalaya, Bihar and Assam were each lower in every round. [C29]

## Act 5 · What goes with fewer babies? Our analysis (7:00 to 9:00)

> So what do the lower-fertility places have in common? [F] We tested two things ourselves, using figures in the same NFHS-6 fact sheets. This part is our analysis, not the survey's. [C31]
> First, the share of women aged 15 to 49 with 10 or more years of schooling. [C31]
> Second, the share of women aged 20 to 24 who were married before 18. [C32]
> Across all 35 places, where more women had 10 or more years of schooling, fertility tends to be lower. The correlation is minus 0.36. [C31]
> In Kerala, 87% of women had 10 or more years of schooling, and 3% of women aged 20 to 24 were married before 18. [C33]
> In Bihar, the figures are 33% and 35%. [C34]
> For child marriage, the link is weaker: plus 0.29. With only 35 places, a link that weak could partly be chance. [C32]
> Next, compare with NFHS-5. On the same 35 places, the schooling link was minus 0.52, and the child marriage link was plus 0.46. [C35]
> So both links are weaker in NFHS-6. [C36]
> Part of that comes from one small island territory. Lakshadweep, where 1,150 women were interviewed, is among the highest in schooling and reads 2.2 on fertility. [C61] [C57]
> Leave it out, and the schooling link is minus 0.46, against minus 0.50 in NFHS-5. [C61]
> So the link has softened a little. It has not disappeared. [C36]
> A word of caution. [F] This is a pattern, not proof that one thing causes the other. [C37]
> Many other things differ between states, and we did not test them. [C37]

**ON SCREEN:** scatter plot, one dot per place: x = women 15-49 with 10+ years of schooling (%), y = TFR; trend line; Kerala and Bihar labelled; caption "Our analysis. Correlation: -0.36 (NFHS-5, same 35 places: -0.52). Pattern, not proof." Second scatter for child marriage (+0.29; NFHS-5: +0.46); Lakshadweep ringed with "1,150 women interviewed".

## Act 6 · The twist (9:00 to 10:00)

> Here is the twist. [F] Between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5, of the 33 places that can be compared, Manipur included, 14 fell by 0.3 or more on the printed figures. None rose by that much. The other 19 were about the same. Two read slightly higher: Kerala, from 1.6 to 1.8, and Tamil Nadu, from 1.7 to 1.8. [C38]
> NFHS-6 looks different. [F] In 16 of the 35 places, NFHS-6 is higher than NFHS-5. In 12 it is lower, and in 7 it is the same. [C39]
> But look closer. [F] In 10 of those 16, the difference is just 0.1, and we treat that as about the same. [C59] [C49]
> On the printed figures, six places are 0.3 or more higher, and six are 0.3 or more lower. [C58]
> Among places with bigger samples, the largest rise is Jammu and Kashmir: 1.4 in NFHS-5, 1.8 in NFHS-6. [C40]
> Nagaland fell by a full child per woman between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5, from 2.7 to 1.7. In NFHS-6 it reads 2.0, about 0.3 higher. [C41]
> A few small places also show jumps, but their samples run from about 800 to 2,200 women, so we leave them out. [C42] [C48] [C57]
> Kerala reads 1.8 again, and Tamil Nadu 1.7. [C43] [C44] Both are about the same. [C49]
> So is India's fall levelling off? [F] One survey cannot tell us. The NFHS-6 numbers are provisional, and only a later survey can show whether this is a turn or just noise. [C47] [C56]

For NFHS-4 to NFHS-5, a bar "14 of 33 fell by 0.3 or more; 19 about the same; none rose by 0.3" with the note "Manipur included (in NFHS-5, not in NFHS-6)".

## Act 7 · So what? (10:00 to 11:00)

> Why does this matter? [F] The United Nations notes that in countries below replacement level, population ageing speeds up. And if fertility keeps falling, then over the long run the share of people of working age shrinks, and the share of older people grows. [C50]
> NFHS-6 already shows different age profiles. In Kerala, 20.7% of people are 60 or older. In Bihar, 11.5%. For India as a whole, 12.9%. [C60]
> At the other end, 35.6% of people in Bihar are under 15, against 20.3% in Kerala. [C60]
> The table shows how much, not why. [C51]
> So where does your state sit on the map, and what do you see in your own family? Tell us in the comments. [F]
> Every number is in the Data Receipt below. [C53]
> And there is a new India map on Data Kadai to look at next. [C54]
