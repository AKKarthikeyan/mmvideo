## Verdict on the premise
**"Stronger" does not hold as stated.** The honest version: **the Bay of Bengal makes about twice as many cyclones as the Arabian Sea, and far deadlier ones, but there is no sign they are stronger per storm.**
- Count: about 2:1 since 1982 (122 vs 50 cyclonic storms by IMD winds; IMD's own annual table 120 vs 51) [C001]. IMD's familiar "4:1" describes the long record, 1891-1995 [C002].
- Per storm: 62% of cyclonic storms in each sea became severe, 1982-2024 by IMD winds, and the US JTWC's winds show the same (40% vs 41% reach Category 1 or more) [C003]; no detectable difference. Over IMD's whole record a smaller share of Bay cyclones turned severe (46% vs 62%) [C046].
- Since 2004 the Arabian Sea has matched the Bay in extremely severe storms (10 vs 10) and had more super cyclonic storms (2 vs 1) by IMD's count [C004]; Arabian Sea cyclonic storms rose 52% in 2001-2019 [C005].
- What still favours the Bay: it has more strong storms by sheer number, it holds the north Indian Ocean intensity record (Odisha 1999, about 140 knots, 912 hPa) [C008], and by the US JTWC's winds 8 of 9 Category-5-strength storms since 1982 were Bay storms [C007] (IMD rates most of them lower: conflict K2).
- **Deadlier:** WMO's record for the deadliest tropical cyclone ever is the Bay storm of 12-13 November 1970 in coastal Bangladesh, an estimated 300,000 deaths [C010], mostly from storm surge [C011].
- Suggested honest titles (AK decides): "Why the Bay of Bengal makes the deadliest cyclones"; "Why the Bay of Bengal gets twice the cyclones"; "There is no sign Bay of Bengal cyclones are stronger per storm. So why are they deadlier?"

## The real story, in plain words
- **A small cyclone basin.** The north Indian Ocean makes only about 5-7% of the world's tropical cyclones, about five a year [C009]. Most form before the monsoon (May) and after it (October-November, peak November) [C039].
- **Why more in the Bay.**
  - It is warmer than the Arabian Sea in every month (1991-2020: 28.6 C vs 28.0 C on average) [C025]; IMD says the colder Arabian Sea "inhibits the formation and intensification" of storms [C025].
  - It gets second-hand storms: remnants of Pacific typhoons cross the South China Sea into the Bay [C023]. Since 1982 about 1 in 12 Bay cyclones began as a Pacific system (Bulbul 2019 from Typhoon Matmo, for example) [C024]. The 1999 super cyclone itself began as a South China Sea low [C023].
  - Bay storms that cross India mostly die over land, so few reach the Arabian Sea (IMD FAQ Q31) [C023].
- **The monsoon paradox.** In June-September the Bay spawns about seven times as many depressions as the Arabian Sea (622 vs 89 since 1891), yet almost none become cyclones: 2 in July-August across 1982-2024 [C037]. Monsoon wind shear tears them apart, and they hit land within a day or two [C038]. The months when the Bay is warmest compared with the Arabian Sea are its quietest [C025, C037].
- **The river lid.** After the monsoon, river water and rain leave a shallow fresh layer over a deep warm layer in the north Bay [C026]; about 3,050 km3 of fresh water arrives north of 14N in one monsoon [C029]. A cyclone normally stirs up cold water and weakens itself. Here it can't: pre-monsoon cyclones cooled the sea by about 3 C, post-monsoon north-Bay cyclones by about 1 C [C027]. At one station scientists calculated that stirring would *warm* the surface by 0.7 C [C030]. A model study gives the fresh layer about 40% of this effect and deep warm water about 60% [C045]. Worldwide, storms over such "barrier layers" intensified nearly 50% faster [C031]. The 1999 storm went from Category 2 to 5 in 30 hours [C032].
- **Why deadlier: the surge.** Storm surge causes about 90% of cyclone deaths, India's disaster authority says [C014]. At the head of the Bay everything that makes surge worse comes together: shallow coastal water, the Bay narrowing like a funnel (our analogy), high tides, crowded low islands and the many mouths of the Ganga-Brahmaputra-Meghna [C033]. The Bay narrows from about 1,400 km wide at 16N to under 500 km at 21.5N [C034]. IMD rates West Bengal and Bangladesh as able to see 10-13 m surges, against 2-3 m for Gujarat [C035]. The 1970 surge is listed as 13 m by IMD and as 10 m on a 2006 Bangladesh-government workshop slide (from the telecom regulator, not a weather or disaster agency, so not an official Bangladeshi measurement) [C013]. India's east coast took 308 cyclone landfalls in 1891-2006 against 48 on the west coast [C036].
- **The toll, and the turn.** 1970: at least 300,000 dead (WMO) [C010]. Nargis 2008 in Myanmar: 84,537 dead and 53,836 missing (official) [C016]. Then Amphan 2020, a super cyclonic storm: 98 deaths in West Bengal (IMD), after more than 3 million people were evacuated across India and Bangladesh (WMO) [C022].

## Fresh angles (gate G7)
G7: PASS. Verified facts that popular explainers leave out or get wrong (myths.md lists what they say):
1. **No sign that Bay cyclones are stronger per storm** [C003, C046]: explainers and our own calendar title say "stronger"; IMD's own numbers say the Bay makes more, not fiercer (62% vs 62% turn severe since 1982, and JTWC agrees; 46% vs 62% over IMD's whole record, where early undercounting may play a part). At the very top JTWC favours the Bay (Category 5: 8 vs 1, a small count; C007). Usable as the hook, worded "no sign", not "false".
2. **The Arabian Sea has caught up at the top end** [C004, C005]: since 2004, 10 extremely severe storms each (IMD).
3. **A storm can't cool the north Bay, and can even warm it** [C027, C030, C045]: explainers mention a fresh-water "lid" but not that stirring can warm the surface, or that deep warm water does more than half the work. Usable as the "why" answer.
4. **The monsoon paradox** [C037, C038, C025]: seven times the monsoon depressions, almost no monsoon cyclones, in the months the Bay is warmest.
5. **The deadliest-cyclone lists rest on shaky numbers** [C019, C015]: the 1737 "300,000" traces to a London magazine printing reports six months late; the East India Company's official in Calcutta reported "near 3000" for Calcutta alone, and Bilham allows 300,000 might cover the wider delta. So the toll is DISPUTED (about 3,000 in Calcutta to 300,000; a model gives 35,000 for the region), yet the 300,000 is still in India's 2008 national guidelines.
6. **Deaths have collapsed** [C022]: at least 300,000 in 1970 vs about a hundred in India for Amphan, a higher IMD class.
