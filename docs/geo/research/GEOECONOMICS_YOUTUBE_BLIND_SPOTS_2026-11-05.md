## The authority-engagement paradox
Channels with the deepest institutional expertise on geoeconomic mechanics draw a negligible audience; high-velocity channels rely on macro abstraction, dramatic forecasts and doom tropes. Four tiers:
1. **Mass-market geopolitical and economic infotainment** (Zeihan on Geopolitics, RealLifeLore, CaspianReport, Economics Explained, OBF, Maxinomics, Think School): geographic determinism, demographic collapse, energy crises, trade wars, bankruptcy rankings. 950K+ subscribers; 2M–13M views for viral explainers; 327.8M total channel views (Zeihan). Limit: provocative macro forecasting over granular supply-chain mechanics.
2. **Legacy broadcast and news** (Al Jazeera English, CBS News, DW News): kinetic conflict, oil shocks, US foreign policy, headline sanctions. 6.2M–17.5M subscribers; 3.1B–6.8B views. Reactive; rarely covers sub-tier industrial bottlenecks.
3. **Independent multipolars and macro commentators** (Geopolitical Economy Report, Lena Petrova (WAI), Inside China Business, PGurus, Dangerously Informed): BRICS, de-dollarisation, US-China tech rivalry. 65.6K average views (Petrova); 699K subscribers and 81.5M views (GER); 8K–48K for niche deep dives. Often a binary "West in decline vs rising Global South" lens.
4. **Think tanks and academic forums** (CFR, Atlantic Council GeoEconomics Center, Stanford GSB, Hoover, FPRI, ORF Delhi, FIIA): Bretton Woods reform, sovereign debt, financial coercion. 350–4,400 views for specialised panels. Example: Matteo Maggiori's talk on financial muscle drew 1,800–4,400 views; Atlantic Council streams from the Oct 2026 IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok drew under 1,000 each.

Mass-market creators treat the economy as a chess game between monolithic blocks and headline commodities, so sub-tier inputs, midstream refining, multimodal logistics friction, wholesale settlement and non-treaty resource diversion stay unexplored.

## 1. Macro: from demand-side doom to supply-side O-ring fragility
**Covered:** fiat collapse and debt doom ("Once You Understand MONEY, You'll Stop Saving It" 2.9M views; "Which Rich Country Goes Bankrupt First?" 8.2M; Ray Dalio debt-cycle warnings 7.4M), "peak globalisation", "slowbalization", "China Shock 2.0".
**Missing: the inelastic supply curve and O-ring geopolitics.** LSE research: after container standardisation (1968–1970) and China's manufacturing value-added rising from ~$0.6T to $4.7T after 2004, the global supply curve was near flat, so demand rose as output not inflation, and a savings glut depressed rates. Governments also muted demand swings with fast fiscal action (the $1.9T American Rescue Plan; ~$4T US outlays by end 2021). Now supply elasticity has eroded. Cross-border asset and liability positions plateaued at 52%–62% of world GDP after 2008. The "Great Reallocation" of US supply chains from China to Vietnam, Mexico and Taiwan came in waves: to 2020 for goods easy to re-source; 2021–2024 for contract-intensive goods; March–August 2025 under tariff and security pressure. In "O-ring" geopolitics the failure of one specialised input destroys a whole network's value, and disturbances start in state decisions (tariffs, energy shocks, chokepoints). The same fragility exists in services on shared digital infrastructure: the Feb 2024 ransomware attack on Change Healthcare, which processes roughly half of US health claims.

## 2. Strategic tech and critical minerals
**Covered:** smartphone weaponisation, AI chip export controls, ASML EUV limits, Taiwan, generic "rare earths".
**Missing 1: Spruce Pine quartz.** Two mines in Spruce Pine, North Carolina, joined to the world by one mountain road, are the sole global source of ultrapure natural quartz for Czochralski crucibles used to melt semiconductor silicon. In late September 2024 Hurricane Helene dropped two feet of rain, collapsed most bridges, cut power and water, and shut the mines; chipmakers as far as East Asia assessed the fallout. Resilience literature separates redundancy (buffers, spare capacity, backup suppliers) from flexibility; at this base layer neither exists.
**Missing 2: midstream metallisation, minor defence metals, heavy rare earths, extraterritorial jurisdiction.** China has about 60% of critical mineral mining and 80% of processing. After 2023 limits on gallium, germanium and graphite, Beijing moved in August 2024 to antimony (YouTube coverage: niche channels around 8,300 views). Antimony hardens lead in batteries and bullets, goes into ammunition primers, flame retardants, night vision, infrared missile sensors, fibre optics, and over 200 munition types. Global reserves exceed 2M tons; China's 2025 mine output ~40,000 t was 36% of 110,000 t global, but China holds 80–90% of refining. After licensing in August 2024 and an outright US ban on 3 December 2024 (antimony, gallium, germanium, superhard materials), shipments to the US fell 97%; spot prices rose about 2,600% ($1,400 to $38,000 a ton; $9.80/lb to $27.50/lb before easing to $14.50/lb). The report says the broader civilian ban was suspended from 9 Nov 2025 to 27 Nov 2026 while the ban on US military end-users stays, that the Pentagon committed nearly $1B in October 2025 to restock, and that Australia named antimony a priority reserve mineral. In April 2025 Beijing put seven heavy rare earths under licensing, notably dysprosium and terbium (magnet stability in jet engines, missile guidance, drone motors). The bottleneck is metallisation, turning separated oxides into high-purity metals and alloys outside China; REalloys (Euclid, Ohio, 80% offtake from North America's only heavy rare earth processor, feedstock from the US, Canada, Brazil, Kazakhstan, Greenland) is racing to close it. In October 2025 Beijing restricted export of rare earth processing technology and asserted jurisdiction over foreign-made products with trace Chinese rare earth content, copying the US Foreign Direct Product Rule.
**Missing 3: Global South beneficiation and seabed governance.** Instead of "neocolonial scramble" framing: Zimbabwe's raw lithium export ban pushed Huayou Cobalt to invest $400M in Africa's first lithium sulphate plant at Arcadia. Deep sea: polymetallic nodules 4–6 km down in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (Hawaii to Mexico), with The Metals Company and Nauru test-mining amid scrutiny of effluent toxicity and ISA legal deadlock.

## 3. Caloric geoeconomics: fertiliser, phosphate, potash
**Covered:** oil, pipelines, renewables. A fertiliser creator's video drew 48,000 views and noted media ignores the geology of nutrition.
**Missing 1: China's phosphate halt and the US critical-mineral listing.** Plants need nitrogen (from natural gas via ammonia), potassium (potash) and phosphorus (phosphate rock, no substitute). US phosphate output (Florida, Idaho, North Carolina) has declined for decades; Canada, Australia and the EU have negligible reserves. China restricted phosphate exports from 2021; in March 2025 exports fell from 950,000 t to 13,000 t in a month; by December 2025 the NDRC, via industry associations, formalised a halt until August 2026. DAP prices rose 28% in a year. On 7 November 2025 the USGS final 2025 critical minerals list added phosphate, uranium and metallurgical coal (60 minerals). Then: Moroccan shipments to the US resumed in August 2026 after duty suspensions (earlier USITC anti-dumping friction from 2020), and on 30 October 2026 the European Commission cleared a joint venture between KAES Phosphate Holdings (KI Group) and Morocco's OCP Nutricrops.
**Missing 2: OCP and phosphate diplomacy.** Morocco holds ~70% of known phosphate rock reserves (including deposits in contested Western Sahara), as world population heads to 9B by 2050 needing 70% more food. OCP Group (Royal Dahir of 26 Jan 1920) became Africa's agricultural lifeline: Africa holds 60–65% of uncultivated arable land; OCP's African share went from near zero to 54%. The OCP Foundation (2007) made soil-fertility maps in six countries, customised blends, trained over 4M farmers, set up local subsidiaries (which also made 27,000 face masks and sanitiser kits in Burkina Faso, Guinea, Madagascar, Togo during Covid). In Ethiopia a £2.6B joint-venture plant of 3.8M tons a year combines Moroccan phosphate with Ethiopian potash and gas; also deals in Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal, Tanzania. Morocco's 2017 readmission to the African Union after 33 years followed, despite Algeria and South Africa. Weakness: OCP imports 1.5–2M tons a year of grey ammonia (ammonia is 2% of global fossil fuel use); phosphates are 9% of Morocco's 2035 mitigation target; the £9.6B Green Investment Strategy (2023–2027) and a MAD 5B raise in June 2026 aim at 100% renewables by 2030 and green hydrogen and ammonia.
**Missing 3: potash sanction arbitrage.** Canada (#1), Russia (#2), Belarus (#3) lead exports. After sanctions on Belarus, Nutrien, Mosaic and Uralkali took some volume. On 19 March 2026 the US lifted sanctions on Belarusian potash (~5% of US demand before), a lever to diversify supply and undercut Canadian pricing during trade talks.

## 4. Trade corridors and nearshoring: ambition versus friction
**Covered:** map lines for BRI, the Middle Corridor, "Mexico nearshoring boom".
**Missing 1: Mexico's electron, water and customs ceilings.** AMPIP names three bottlenecks: electricity, water (esp. Monterrey), customs. Under-investment in CFE transmission leaves the grid at its limit; industrial demand grew over 4% in 2025; a projected 48,000 MW shortfall and $38B grid capex over five years. Energy, not real estate, now gates site choice: pre-secured kVAs carry premiums, on-site generation is needed, and firms must settle an energy strategy with CFE before buying land.
**Missing 2: the Trans-Caspian Middle Corridor (TITR).** Since 2022 shippers sought alternatives to Russia's Northern Corridor; DSV closed its €14.3B ($16.82B) DB Schenker purchase in April 2025 and stopped all rail freight through Russia in August 2025. The Middle Corridor chain: rail across Kazakhstan, Caspian ferry, rail or road via Azerbaijan, Georgia, Turkey. Backing: EU €10B for Central Asian connectivity (January 2024), plus €12B (€3B transport) at the April 2025 EU–Central Asia summit. Gains: Azerbaijan Railways' $250M upgrade of the 184-km Georgian section of Baku-Tbilisi-Kars (13 stations, 55 bridges, 8 substations) lifted capacity from 1M to 5M tons (Turkey targets 17M tons by 2034); Kazakhstan's terminal at the Xian dry port; e-customs at Altynkol-Khorgos cut processing from 24 hours to 30 minutes; EBRD and EU approved €45M ($52.93M) in 2025 for Aktau Port (two container berths, handling 4 to 2.5 min per box); Baku's Alat port expanding to 300,000–500,000 TEU and 25M tons. Volumes: ~4.47M tons in 2024 against 6M capacity (target 10M by 2027), a 14-fold rise in Chinese transit cargo; Aktau–Baku feeder 51,400 TEU (2024) to 59,400 (2025); East-West transit up 55% in the first eight months of 2026.
Frictions: annual container capacity 80,000 TEU, 2024 transit turnover 36,000 TEU (Kazakh targets 50,000 in 2025, 85,000 in 2026–2029), under a tenth of the Russian Northern Route's 381,000 TEU and tiny against 8.6M TEU by deep sea; Trans-Caspian rail freight revenue only $298.04M in 2026. The Caspian is falling in level, limiting vessel loads between Aktau and Baku; feeder vessels, locomotives, wagons and empty-box returns are short. Georgia's Anaklia deep-sea port (a World Bank priority) lost funding, leaving Poti and Batumi overloaded. Marketed block-train times are 12–15 days (Kazakhstan targets 10–14 by 2029, called unrealistic by its railway carriers' association); China hubs (Xian, Yiwu, Zhengzhou) to Baku take 12–14 days, average corridor transit improved to 18–23 days from 38–53; to Ambarli 23–28, Constanta 31–34, Duisburg/Budapest/Milan 32–37, Valencia/Barcelona 44–47. One wrong transit document (TIR Carnet, T1, CMR) can turn 25 days into 35. Kazakhstan (57.8% of Central Asian GDP, 14% of world uranium) stays tied to Russian infrastructure: the Caspian Pipeline carries 79% of its oil exports, and Moscow-linked assets are 25% of Kazakh uranium output.
**Missing 3: Peru's Chancay megaport versus the Bioceanic Railway.** Chancay (80 km north of Lima; opened November 2024 with Xi Jinping) is 60% COSCO Shipping Ports and 40% Volcan (Glencore-controlled), financed by a March 2023 $975M syndicated loan from five Chinese banks. 17.8 m natural depth, two berths over 400 m, the only west-coast South American port for 24,000-TEU ships; direct service to Shanghai in 23 days, 12–15 days faster than transshipping via Mexico or the US. Peru amended its port law in June 2024 so COSCO keeps exclusive operating rights. First year (Nov 2024–Nov 2025): over 286,000 TEU, beating a 200,000 target, Peru's fourth-largest container port, with regional transshipment; it also lowers costs for South Korea and prompted talk of updating the 2011 Korea–Peru FTA. The 4,000–4,900 km Peru–Brazil Bioceanic Railway (Ilhéus via FIOL/FICO across the Amazon and Andes to Chancay) is largely aspirational: Brazil and China signed a joint-study agreement in July 2025; under 15% of the needed Brazilian rail exists; only the 537-km FIOL 1 (Ilhéus–Caetité) is done, the 485-km FIOL 2 to Barreiras is under construction, and Transnordestina took about two decades. The Andean alignment is in a five-year preparatory phase. It would cross the Amazon "Deforestation Arc" (75% of Amazon deforestation) and threaten the Xingu headwaters and Xingu Indigenous Park, under Brazil's August 2025 licensing framework and Indigenous consultation. Economists see it as a geopolitical bet, not a cost-minimising freight route.

## 5. Financial plumbing and non-kinetic infrastructure
**Covered:** "death of the dollar", petrodollar, BRICS gold currency, sanctions on Russian reserves, Houthi attacks.
**Missing 1: wholesale ledger fork, mBridge versus Agorá.** Project mBridge began in 2019 as Inthanon-LionRock (HKMA and Bank of Thailand); in February 2021 the PBoC's Digital Currency Institute and the Central Bank of the UAE joined, under the BIS Innovation Hub in Hong Kong. It is a multi-CBDC ledger (HotStuff+ consensus, EVM-compatible); each central bank runs a validating node, issues wholesale CBDC and settles atomic payment-versus-payment FX in seconds, cutting costs by up to half. A 2022 six-week pilot: 20 commercial banks, $22M, 164 transactions in e-HKD, e-CNY, e-AED, e-THB. MVP in June 2024; the report says it processed $55.5B of cross-border payments (UAE firms paying Chinese suppliers in e-CNY). On 31 October 2024 the BIS exited and handed mBridge to the four central banks; then-General Manager Agustín Carstens said mBridge is "not the BRICS bridge" and BIS products must not be conduits for sanctions evasion. The BIS then moved to Project Agorá: eight central banks and more than 40 private financial institutions testing tokenised commercial bank deposits and tokenised reserves on one ledger. The real multipolar story is a fork between a PBoC/Gulf/Asian central-bank CBDC rail and a BIS/Western public-private tokenised-deposit rail.
**Missing 2: subsea cable repair deficit.** Installation and maintenance is concentrated among four firms: SubCom (US, 11.6–12%), Alcatel Submarine Networks (France, ~10–13%), Optic Marine Services (10%). Operators cannot afford dedicated standby ships, so resilience relies on shared regional fleets. Around 2022 SubCom stopped global maintenance and repair to focus on new installation. With Red Sea security and South China Sea permitting problems, repairs can take months.
**Missing 3: Qosh Tepa Canal.** The Amu Darya (2,525 m3/s) runs along Tajikistan, Afghanistan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan toward the Aral remnants. Proposed in the 1970s under Mohammad Daoud Khan, revived with USAID feasibility studies in 2020 (first 7 km dug before the August 2021 Taliban takeover), made a flagship by the de facto authorities in early 2022: 285 km long, 80 m wide, 8.5 m deep from Kaldar (Balkh) via Jowzjan to Andkhoy (Faryab), to irrigate 550,000 ha, completion about 2028. For the Taliban government (sanctioned, unrecognised, hit by its April 2022 poppy ban that cut cultivation from 233,000 ha in 2022 to 10,800 ha in 2023) it is a tool of legitimacy, jobs and wheat self-sufficiency. Water allocation rests on Soviet Protocol 566 of 1987 dividing 61.50 bcm a year among four Soviet republics (29.60 bcm to Uzbekistan, most of the rest to Turkmenistan), excluding Afghanistan, which historically used about 2%. Afghanistan has signed no regional or international water treaty; the canal could take 20–25% of the flow (~668.4 m3/s). Assessments say Uzbekistan could lose nearly 250,000 agricultural jobs and the Aral crisis worsen; Tashkent and Ashgabat would need technical talks on seasonal withdrawals without formal recognition. Engineering: earthen walls and unlined floor; in December 2023 Sentinel-2 imagery showed an embankment breach that spilled for about a month, making a 9-km water body over 30 km2; seepage raises groundwater and pulls salt to the surface, risking lasting salinisation.
