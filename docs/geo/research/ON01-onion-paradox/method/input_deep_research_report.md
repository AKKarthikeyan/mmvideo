# The Onion Paradox: Why Does One Vegetable Have So Much Power Over India?

## Executive summary

The strongest **Indica Atlas** version of this documentary is not “why onions become expensive”. It is a map-led story about how **geography, seasonality and trade policy turn one semi-perishable crop into a national pressure point**. Culture and religion work best as a brief human-interest entry: onions are deeply embedded in many Indian cuisines, yet are deliberately excluded in Jain practice and in some Hindu sattvic, Vaishnava and temple-food traditions. Those distinctions matter, because “Hindus do not eat onions” is plainly wrong; dietary practice varies by community, region and religious context. Academic work on South Indian foodways likewise shows substantial variation rather than one uniform Hindu rule. citeturn867761search0turn867761search2turn867761search3turn867761search7

The geographic concentration is striking. In the Department of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare's **2024–25 third advance estimate**, India produced about **30.77 million tonnes** of onion on about **1.97 million hectares**. Maharashtra alone was estimated at **13.56 million tonnes**, roughly **44% of national production**, followed by Madhya Pradesh at about **4.44 million tonnes, 14%**, and Karnataka at about **2.58 million tonnes, 8%**. Maharashtra accounted for almost half of onion acreage. That makes the national market unusually exposed to weather, storage conditions and logistics in a relatively small set of producing belts. citeturn937457search7turn937457search0

The key temporal fact is just as important as the map. India grows onions in **kharif, late-kharif and rabi** seasons, but ICAR-DOGR research describes the rabi crop harvested around April–May as roughly **60% of annual output** and the crop with the best storage characteristics. The kharif and late-kharif crops together make up the remainder and are generally less suitable for long storage. This means India's onion system depends on storing part of the spring rabi harvest through the hot and humid months until fresh kharif arrivals become available later in the year. citeturn847829search8turn847829search0

That storage bridge is fragile. ICAR sources document substantial physical loss from dehydration, rotting and sprouting under conventional conditions; one ICAR description says losses can reach **30–40% during storage**, while experimental comparisons show very large differences between conventional ventilated structures and improved controlled systems. These figures should not be treated as a universal loss rate for every farmer or every year, but they establish why onion inventory is not equivalent to storing wheat or rice. citeturn847829search1turn847829search7

The supply chain therefore has an unusual shape: **production is geographically concentrated, demand is geographically dispersed, and inventory physically decays while being held**. Government data make this spatial structure visible. AGMARKNET publishes daily mandi-level minimum, maximum and modal prices, while the Department of Consumer Affairs monitors wholesale and retail prices across hundreds of centres. This gives the film a powerful visual device: trace a bulb from Nashik or another producing belt into a mandi auction, into storage, and then by road or rail to Delhi, Chennai, Kolkata or other consumption centres. citeturn885443search0turn885443search2turn932081search3

The export story turns the onion into regional geopolitics. APEDA/DGCIS data show that India exported roughly **1.55 million tonnes of fresh onions worth about US$397 million in FY2025–26**, with major destinations including **Malaysia, Sri Lanka, the UAE, Nepal, Bangladesh, Qatar and Kuwait**. Because nearby import-dependent markets buy Indian onions, Indian export restrictions can transmit a domestic food-price intervention across borders. citeturn937457search0turn937457search1

India's export policy has repeatedly changed when domestic supplies became tight. The government prohibited most onion exports from **8 December 2023 to 3 May 2024**, then reopened them subject to a **US$550/tonne minimum export price and a 40% export duty** from May 2024. On **13 September 2024**, the MEP was abolished and the duty cut to 20%; that remaining 20% duty was removed effective **1 April 2025**. I found no later onion-specific restriction in the official DGFT/APEDA notification index through **12 November 2026**. The defensible current-status wording is therefore: **ordinary fresh-onion exports appear to remain free of a ban, MEP and onion-specific export duty as of 12 November 2026, based on the latest instruments located; this should be rechecked immediately before publication because onion policy can change by notification with immediate effect.** citeturn932081search6turn932081search2turn932081search0

The political mythology requires care. Onion prices were unquestionably a major political issue during the **1998 Delhi Assembly election**: contemporaneous reporting documented retail prices around ₹50–70/kg in Delhi, Parliament recorded huge year-on-year onion price increases, and the government subsidised onion distribution after crop damage and delayed arrivals. Congress then defeated the BJP in Delhi. But that sequence does **not** prove that onions alone caused the election result. The sound documentary formulation is: *onions became a highly visible symbol of the government's cost-of-living problem and were widely treated as an electoral liability, but electoral causation cannot be isolated from other political factors.* citeturn847829search6turn847829search10turn847829search11turn105827search0

The farmer-consumer paradox may be the film's most important insight. When harvests are abundant, farmers may face distress prices precisely because fresh onions are difficult and costly to hold; months later, consumers may face sharply higher prices if stored inventory has deteriorated or the next crop is damaged. Government policy therefore oscillates between two legitimate objectives: **protecting consumers from scarcity and protecting growers from price collapse**. The Price Stabilisation Fund, buffer procurement and releases, export controls and logistics interventions all sit inside that tension. citeturn885443search28turn932081search2turn932081search3

**The map-led thesis:** India does not have an “onion problem” because it consumes too many onions. It has a recurring onion problem because a nationwide appetite depends disproportionately on a few production landscapes, three harvest windows, months of risky storage, long-distance logistics and a government forced to arbitrate continuously between farmers, consumers and neighbouring markets. citeturn937457search7turn847829search8turn932081search3

## Culture, history and the human entry point

Onion has a long history in South Asia, but claims such as “India has eaten onions for 5,000 years” should be avoided unless tied to specific archaeological evidence. Archaeobotanical work reports *Allium cepa* remains from sites including Raja-Nala-Ka-Tila in Uttar Pradesh, dated broadly to around **1300–700 BCE**, while broader palaeobotanical reviews place onion among several culinary plants documented in ancient Indian archaeological contexts. That establishes antiquity, but not a single uninterrupted culinary tradition across all regions of India. citeturn867761search10turn867761search8

A safer historical line for narration is: **“Onions were known in the subcontinent in antiquity, but the cuisines that use—or reject—them evolved differently across regions and communities.”** A socio-historical review of Indian cuisine emphasises precisely this diversity and the cumulative influence of geography, religion, trade and political history on regional foodways. citeturn867761search6

Jain practice offers the clearest religious contrast. Jain dietary doctrine commonly excludes underground vegetables such as onion, garlic and potatoes because harvesting them destroys the plant and, in Jain understandings, may harm multiple forms of life. Yet practice is not perfectly uniform across every sect: scholarship on the Terāpanth tradition documents historical exceptions and internal debates. The documentary should therefore say **“many Jain traditions avoid onions and other root vegetables”**, not “all Jains never eat onions”. citeturn867761search9turn867761search0

Among Hindus, the picture is still more heterogeneous. Some sattvic, Vaishnava, Brahmin and temple-food traditions avoid onion and garlic, while millions of Hindu households use them routinely. Research on South Indian vegetarian food traditions links the avoidance of onion and garlic to particular theological and dietary systems, while scholarship on South Indian foodways documents differences even within Brahmin communities. citeturn867761search2turn867761search3turn867761search7

For **Indica Atlas**, keep this cultural section to roughly one minute. Its purpose is to establish the paradox: **one bulb can be indispensable in one kitchen, ritually excluded in another, and economically strategic at national scale.** The documentary should then pivot immediately to the map.

## Geography, seasons and the supply chain

The production map is the backbone of the film. The strongest reproducible official table is DA&FW's **Agricultural Statistics at a Glance 2024–25**, Table 2.50(b). Its third advance estimate puts national onion area at roughly **1.97 million hectares** and output at **30.77 million tonnes**. Maharashtra's estimated **978,160 hectares** and **13.558 million tonnes** correspond to about **49% of acreage and 44% of production**; Madhya Pradesh's roughly **218,270 hectares and 4.443 million tonnes** represent about **11% and 14%**; Karnataka's roughly **195,040 hectares and 2.576 million tonnes** about **10% and 8%**. citeturn937457search7

Those figures also reveal an important analytical point: acreage concentration is even stronger than production concentration. Madhya Pradesh's estimated yield in the same table, about **20.4 tonnes/ha**, is substantially above Maharashtra's roughly **13.9 tonnes/ha**, showing that Maharashtra's dominance arises partly from enormous cultivated area rather than uniquely high yield. citeturn937457search7

The latest national estimate I located says 2024–25 onion output eventually reached about **30.77 million tonnes**, up sharply from **24.27 million tonnes in 2023–24**, while area rose from about 1.54 million to 1.97 million hectares. This is one reason the film should display the **data vintage on-screen**: first, second and third advance estimates can differ materially. citeturn937457search5turn937457search7

Nashik is central because it combines cultivation, storage and market infrastructure. Maharashtra's official agriculture portal now hosts a **district-wise 2025–26 third forecast dataset**, but the search-accessible version does not expose the Nashik onion row in machine-readable form. For a final animated district map, that row should be pulled directly from the downloadable spreadsheet/PDF rather than reconstructed from press reports. A February 2025 report citing district agriculture officials placed total Nashik onion acreage at about **329,000 hectares in 2024–25**, up sharply from the previous season, but this is secondary reporting and should not replace the official district file in the final graphic. citeturn857029search0turn857029search1

The crop calendar explains why one bad weather event can matter months later. ICAR-DOGR characterises Indian onion output as approximately **20% kharif, 20% late-kharif and 60% rabi**. Kharif onions are generally harvested around October–November, late-kharif around February–March and rabi around April–May, with regional variation. The rabi crop is the principal storability crop; it is carried forward to bridge supply until later crops arrive. citeturn847829search8

That seasonal architecture makes monsoon risk asymmetric. Heavy rain can damage standing kharif crops and delay arrivals just when old rabi stocks have been sitting in storage for months. High heat increases physiological weight loss, while high humidity increases rotting; falling temperatures later can stimulate sprouting. NHRDF and ICAR both emphasise curing, ventilation and variety choice as major determinants of storage survival. citeturn847829search2turn847829search1

The supply-chain animation should therefore run:

**Nashik farm → curing → village/on-farm storage → Lasalgaon or other APMC auction → trader/wholesaler → truck or rail corridor → metropolitan wholesale market → retailer → household.**

AGMARKNET provides daily mandi observations by state, district, market, commodity and variety, including minimum, maximum and modal wholesale prices. The Consumer Affairs Price Monitoring System provides the other end of the chain: city-level and all-India retail/wholesale monitoring. These datasets are ideal for an animated **farm-to-city price spread** rather than relying on anecdotal retailer quotes. citeturn885443search0turn885443search5turn885443search2

A particularly Indica Atlas visual is the government's **Kanda Express**. In August 2026 the Department of Consumer Affairs said onion-buffer movement was being organised by rail and road from Nashik to major consumption centres. It reported that 14 railway rakes carried nearly **12,000 tonnes** in 2024–25 and 86 rakes around **88,000 tonnes** to 16 cities in 2025–26; the 2026 programme included Delhi, Chennai, Kolkata, Ernakulam, Guwahati, Lucknow, Patna, Chandigarh and others. This turns onion inflation into literal economic geography: the state tries to flatten regional scarcity by moving physical inventory across the subcontinent. citeturn932081search3turn932081search4

## Trade, politics and the farmer-consumer paradox

India's export map is highly regional. APEDA says FY2025–26 fresh-onion exports were around **1.548 million tonnes worth US$397.24 million**, with Malaysia, Sri Lanka, UAE, Nepal, Bangladesh, Qatar and Kuwait among the major destinations. Independent UN Comtrade-derived data for calendar 2025 likewise show Malaysia, Sri Lanka, UAE, Bangladesh and Nepal among the top markets, which broadly corroborates the regional pattern. citeturn937457search0turn937457search4

That geography means Indian food policy can become foreign economic policy almost overnight. When India restricts onion exports, the intended beneficiary is the domestic consumer, but the same action reduces supply to importing neighbours and Gulf or Southeast Asian markets. Conversely, when export restrictions are relaxed, Indian growers and traders gain access to overseas demand while policymakers accept some risk of firmer domestic prices. This is an analytical inference from the trade structure and policy instruments, not evidence that every export restriction produces the same price effect. citeturn937457search0turn932081search8

The recent policy sequence is unusually cinematic:

| Policy stage | Verified action |
|---|---|
| 8 Dec 2023–3 May 2024 | Export prohibition on most onions |
| 4 May 2024 | Exports reopened with US$550/tonne MEP plus 40% export duty |
| 13 Sep 2024 | MEP removed; export duty reduced to 20% |
| 1 Apr 2025 | Remaining 20% export duty removed |
| 12 Nov 2026 | No later onion-specific restriction found in the official notification index; treat “free export” as latest verified status, subject to same-day recheck |

This timeline is directly supported by DGFT and Consumer Affairs records. citeturn932081search6turn932081search2turn932081search0

The same policy tension appears domestically. In August 2026 the government began releasing buffer onions and selling them through NCCF, NAFED, Kendriya Bhandar and other channels at **₹35/kg**, while simultaneously reporting robust exports and comfortable availability. That is a useful case study because it shows that government intervention is not confined to bans: procurement, buffer storage, subsidised retail and logistics are alternative stabilisation tools. citeturn932081search3turn932081search4

The **1998 Delhi story** deserves its own myth-busting beat. Contemporary Parliament records show onion prices escalating dramatically during 1998; government documents attribute the crisis partly to unfavourable climate, heavy rainfall, crop damage and delayed arrivals, and record subsidised distribution by NAFED. Contemporary election reporting called onion prices a major campaign issue. Congress subsequently displaced the BJP in Delhi. citeturn847829search10turn847829search11turn847829search6turn105827search0

But the line “onions brought down the Delhi government” is too strong. An academic study of weekly food prices from 1993–2012 finds evidence of a distinctive **political price cycle in onions**, suggesting unusual electoral salience, but it does not identify the 1998 Delhi outcome as a single-variable causal effect. The film should distinguish *salience* from *causation*. citeturn847829search9

The farmer-consumer paradox follows directly from perishability. A bumper harvest can produce low mandi prices because growers cannot costlessly wait; a later shortage can produce retail spikes because stock deteriorates and new arrivals are delayed. India's Price Stabilisation Fund guidelines explicitly recognise steep harvest-time price falls and later volatility in horticultural commodities including onions. citeturn885443search28turn847829search1

This is also why **“middlemen cause onion inflation”** is too crude. Traders and storers can have market power, and competition issues deserve investigation, but they also assume real risks: spoilage, working-capital costs, transport costs and uncertain future prices. The stronger documentary question is not “who is cheating whom?” but **“who bears the risk of holding a crop that physically shrinks while everyone waits for the next harvest?”** Storage-loss evidence and official price-stabilisation policy support that framing. citeturn847829search1turn847829search2turn885443search28

## Packaging for Indica Atlas

The strongest title is **“Why Does One Vegetable Have So Much Power Over India?”** because it promises a geographic and political explanation rather than another inflation explainer. A more explicit alternative is **“The Onion Paradox: The Crop That Connects India's Farms, Cities and Borders”**. Three further evidence-faithful options are **“Why India's Onion Prices Keep Becoming a National Crisis”**, **“How Nashik's Onions Reach—and Shake—the Rest of India”**, and **“The Geography Behind India's Onion Problem”**. Those titles are creative framing, while their factual premise rests on the documented geographic concentration, seasonal storage dependence and policy interventions. citeturn937457search7turn847829search8turn932081search3
