## The real story in plain words

**The news.** On 21 October 2026 a Northwestern team led by Gerald Gabrielse published in *Physical Review Letters* [C002] that it had built a "one-positron quantum cyclotron" [C001]. One positron, the electron's antimatter twin, has been kept in a Penning trap for months [C003], at about 0.3 degrees above absolute zero [C004], in a 5.6-tesla magnetic field [C005]. They then kicked it up one energy step at a time with microwaves and watched each step without disturbing it [C012].

**The problem.** Dirac's theory said every electron has a mirror twin with opposite charge, and that the two destroy each other on contact [C030]. Anderson photographed one in cosmic rays and announced it in September 1932 [C032]. Dirac even wondered aloud, in 1933, whether some stars might be made of antimatter [C031]. Physicists think the Big Bang left just one extra matter particle per billion [C033], and nobody knows why. Antimatter is very hard to study because anything it touches destroys it: it must be kept away from all matter, in extreme vacuum [C034].

**The chase.**
1. **1936, Eindhoven: a gauge, not a trap.** Frans Michel Penning, at Philips [C040], filed a patent in 1936 [C041] for a device to measure very low gas pressures [C043]. The old mercury gauge was slow and inaccurate [C045]. His trick was a magnetic field set across the electric field, which stopped electrons from flying straight to the electrode and sent them on long looping paths [C044]. He was measuring vacuum, not catching particles [C043].
2. **A chalk dot in Göttingen.** Hans Dehmelt later wrote that a lecturer, Richard Becker, drew a dot on a blackboard and said "Here is an electron…", and he wondered how anyone could really hold one still [C051]. He told the same story to the Seattle Times on the morning he won the Nobel, adding "but nobody had ever isolated one" [C051]. He had already noticed the Penning gauge's geometry [C052].
3. **1959, Seattle.** With the department's glassblower, Jake Jonson, Dehmelt built his first trap of this kind and held electrons for about 10 seconds [C053], using a design from J. R. Pierce's 1949 book [C054]. He later wrote that he named it a Penning trap, because it derives from Penning's 1936 discharge tube [C064]. The Nobel committee says the Penning trap was developed at the same time in Wolfgang Paul's lab in Bonn [C060]. It took, he wrote, 17 years of work before one electron could be held in it [C094].
4. **1973: one electron alone** [C055]. Dehmelt named a single trapped electron "geonium", an "atom" whose binding is to an apparatus on the Earth [C056]. His car's licence plate read SEPARIS, for "single elementary particle at rest in space" [C095].
5. **1981–87: Priscilla.** Seattle first trapped antimatter in a Penning trap in 1981 [C058, C093]. On 15 September 1984 the lab announced: "Here, right now, in the center of our Penning trap resides positron Priscilla … for the last 3 months!" [C090, C091]. Dehmelt said the name fit because she "never had the chance of trading places with a passing twin" [C092]. By 1987 they compared single electrons and single positrons in the same trap, equal to about two parts in a trillion [C059], using classical cyclotron motion [C099].
6. **1985: ten months.** In Dehmelt's lab a single electron stayed in the trap for more than 10 months. The first author was a young Gerald Gabrielse [C057, C070].
7. **1986–1990: antimatter in the cage.** Gabrielse's team caught antiprotons in a Penning trap at CERN in July 1986, in one 24-hour attempt, holding them up to ten minutes [C071, C073], and kept them for 2 months by 1990 [C072].
8. **2026.** Now at Northwestern [C070], Gabrielse does for one positron what his group did for one electron: hold it in its quantum ground state and read its energy levels one step at a time [C001, C012, C017].

**How it works (the reveal).** A Penning trap is "a container without walls" [C018]: a strong magnetic field keeps the particle circling, and a weak electric field from the end caps pushes it back each time it comes too close, so it bobs up and down in the middle and never touches anything [C010]. To load it, a weak radioactive sodium-22 source fires positrons at up to 546 keV [C006, C009]; a tungsten crystal slows them; one picks up an electron and drifts through a 0.6 mm hole into the trap, where the field strips the electron away and the positron is caught [C007]. It takes 8 to 11 hours to catch one [C008]. Each energy step shows up as a 3.8 Hz change in the bobbing frequency, which proves there is exactly one positron [C012, C014].

**The catch (twist).** The headline goal, checking whether the positron's magnetism exactly mirrors the electron's, has **not been measured yet**; the team says it "now seems feasible" [C015]. And caging one positron for months is 42 years old: Priscilla, 1984 [C090]. The new thing is quantum control of one positron [C001, C099].

**Where it stands (today).** PUBLISHED [C002]. For scale: the biggest antimatter traps hold about a billion particles, about 10^-18 kg [C035]. Gabrielse: "it's not dangerous. We don't have enough antimatter to make an explosion" [C019]. If the two particles turn out different, he says, "that would be the biggest discovery in physics in the last century" [C020].

## People (three who matter)
- **Frans Michel Penning** (Philips, Eindhoven; born Gorinchem 1894, died Utrecht 6 Dec 1953 [C047]): the gauge [C040, C043, C044]. Nominated for the Nobel Prize in 1944, never awarded [C048].
- **Hans G. Dehmelt** (University of Washington, Seattle; Nobel 1989): turned the gauge's geometry into a trap and held one electron [C050–C057].
- **Gerald Gabrielse** (Seattle 1978–87, Harvard, Northwestern): held one electron for 10 months in 1985 [C057], caught antiprotons in 1986 [C071], one positron in 2026 [C001] [C070].

## Fresh angles (G7)
0. **Priscilla (1984): the 2026 headline already happened once.** One positron, held 3 months in Seattle, named like a pet [C090, C091, C092]. In 2026 its quantum states can finally be read one step at a time [C001, C099]. Strongest twist, and the pairing makes the Now story an invention story.
1. **The trap is named after a vacuum gauge.** Penning patented a way to *measure* low pressure; he was not trying to hold anything [C043, C044]. (Popular shorthand "Penning invented the trap" is wrong: M10.)
2. **Same scientist, 41 years apart.** In 1985 Gabrielse was first author on a paper reporting one *electron* held for more than 10 months [C057]; in 2026 his lab holds its antimatter twin for months [C003]. Strong hook/callback.
3. **"Months" is not the new part.** Gabrielse's own team kept antiprotons for 2 months in 1990 [C072]; CERN's BASE kept antiprotons for 405 days [C080]. The new part is quantum control of one positron [C001]. Strong twist, and it protects the channel from "well, actually" comments.
4. **Single positrons were trapped in the 1980s** [C058, C059]: the "for the first time" headlines (M2) are wrong.
5. **A chalk dot started it**, in Dehmelt's own telling [C051].
6. **It takes 8–11 hours to catch one positron**, from a source weak enough for a university lab [C008, C009].
