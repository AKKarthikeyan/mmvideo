# P03 Headphone jack: dossier (Evergreen, "Why is this made this way?")

**Working title (AK, 28 Oct):** "Why is the headphone jack older than the radio?"
**Episode type:** Evergreen (STORY_FORMAT). Shape: a hidden detail on something you own (the shape of the market outliers AK liked).
**Researcher:** Claude, 28 Oct 2026. Every sentence carries claim IDs from `claims.jsonl`, e.g. `[C002]`. Status: research read and ledgered; **G6 (independent verifier) pending**.

> **Honest wording for AK (hype check).** "Older than the radio" is true of the **jack** itself, and it's a good hook: the word and the device were on telegraph desks in 1877, nearly twenty years before Marconi's 1896 patent [C001, C002, C026]. It is **not** true of everything behind the title: the **headphone plug** is first documented in 1899 [C013], after Marconi's 1896 patent, and the **small 3.5 mm size** first appears in a **1969 standard for radio receivers** [C017]. So the better line is **"the jack is older than radio, and then came back to serve it."** Suggested titles are at the end. AK decides.

## The real story in plain words

**The jack began on a telegraph desk.** By 1877 a telegraph journal called a new Western Union switchboard "of the latest Western Union pattern, commonly known as the 'spring-jack'" [C001]. In 1878 a Western Union engineer explained it: a spring strip with a brass pin pressed against another pin; push a *wedge* carrying the instrument wires between the pins and the main-line circuit is broken [C002]. A wedge, not a round plug. Plugs pushed into holes in metal strips were already how exchanges joined lines [C006, C003].

**1879: the telephone borrows it.** In August 1879 a Chicago inventor, Charles Scribner, filed a patent for a "cut-out or spring-jack switch" [C004, C005]. His patent says that "prior to my invention" operators had joined lines by plugging cords into holes drilled in a metal strip [C006]. His jack is a switch: a plug going into a hole pushes a lever down and breaks a contact [C007]. The same month, two Cincinnati inventors, the Joneses, used "spring-jack sockets for receiving the plug end of a flexible conductor" and "a plain round plug instead of the usual wedge" [C008]. The patent was granted to Scribner only in 1884, four and a half years later [C004].

**1880-1883: the name and the spread.** Scientific American, 10 January 1880, uses the term "jack-knife switch" for the telephone version [C009]. An engineer told his French colleagues in April 1881 that the switch took its name from the knife shape it had at first [C010]. By October 1883 a magazine said every line in a Western Electric exchange had "a spring-jack in the switch-board" [C011].

**1885-1908: the operator wears the first headphones.** In February 1885 a magazine described a "Richards head telephone" for operators: a small telephone on two steel bands [C012]. By 1899 the New York Times could describe an operator's "head receiver" on a flexible band, with a cord ending in a plug "slipped into a jack" [C013]. The 1908 Western Electric catalogue sells plug No. 85, with three conductors, for jack No. 77, as the "Operator's head telephone" plug, $1.50 [C014]. **The tip-and-sleeve plug was already "ordinary" in 1901**, when Clausen patented a better one [C015].

**The small version, built for radio.** The first dated document found that gives a 3.5 mm plug size is a 1969 IEC standard titled "Concentric connectors for audio circuits in radio receivers" [C017] (jack sockets for plugs of 3.5 mm, gauge limits 3.50-3.60 mm). In 1959 Sony sold a pocket radio "complete with battery, earphone (for private listening) and carrying case" [C019] but the ad says nothing about the plug. After trying a test model, Morita's first idea was a second jack so two people could share the music: Sony's own account [C020]; by April 1981 a magazine says pocket stereos "use a miniature 3.5-mm, three-conductor jack and plug" [C021].

**2016.** Apple's iPhone 7 press release says every phone includes a Lightning-to-3.5 mm adapter [C023]. A live report from the launch says Phil Schiller reportedly answered why the jack was removed with "courage" [C024].

## People (three who matter)
- **Charles E. Scribner** (Chicago, Western Electric): the 1879 spring-jack switch [C004-C007].
- **C. E. and W. H. Jones** (Cincinnati): the round plug "instead of the usual wedge" [C008]. (They are often forgotten; their patent is a contemporary of Scribner's.)
- **Henry P. Clausen** (Chicago): the 1901 improvement to "the ordinary plug" [C015].
(Marconi is the yardstick, not a character [C016, C026].)

## Fresh angles (G7)
1. **The jack started on a telegraph desk, not in a phone exchange** [C001, C002]. The 1877 word "spring-jack" is older than Scribner's patent.
2. **Scribner's own patent says plugs and holes came before him** [C006]. The popular "Scribner invented it in 1879" is embellished (M2).
3. **The first "jack" is a switch a plug trips**, not an audio socket [C007].
4. **The tip-and-sleeve plug was "ordinary" before Clausen's 1901 patent** [C015]; M3 is contradicted.
5. **The first headphones with a plug on a jack belonged to telephone operators** [C012-C014]; a three-conductor plug for them was catalogue stock by 1908.
6. **The jack older than radio came back to serve it:** the first dated 3.5 mm size is a radio-receiver standard from 1969 [C017].
7. **The "1950s transistor radio" story has no dated document behind it** [M6]: a Claim Check angle if AK wants one.
