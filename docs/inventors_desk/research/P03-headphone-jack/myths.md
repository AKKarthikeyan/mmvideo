# P03 Headphone jack: the popular version, checked

The popular claims come from the way the story is usually told online (Wikipedia's "Phone connector (audio)" page, read as a finding aid only, and the working title "Why is the headphone jack older than the radio?"). Nothing here enters the script except through claims.jsonl.

| # | Popular claim | Verdict | Claims | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | **The jack is older than the radio** (working title) | Confirmed | C001, C002, C016, C026 | The "spring-jack" was telegraph kit by 1877 and described in a book in 1878; Marconi began in early 1895 (his own words) and his British patent GB 12,039 has a priority date of 2 June 1896. Condition: this is about the *jack*, not the small 3.5 mm size or the headphone plug (see M7, M8) |
| M2 | **Scribner invented the jack in 1879** | Embellished | C001, C004, C005, C006 | He patented a *spring-jack switch* for telephone exchanges. The word and the device were already on telegraph desks (1877, 1878), and his own patent says plugs went into holes before his invention |
| M3 | **Clausen's 1901 patent gave us the modern plug** | Contradicted | C015 | His patent improves "the ordinary plug", and already speaks of its "tip and sleeve" contacts. The tip-and-sleeve plug existed before 1901 |
| M4 | **The jack was made for headphones** | Embellished | C002, C007, C008 | The 1879 jack is a switch that a plug trips. The round plug replaced the telegraph "wedge" by 1879 (Jones). A headset on a plug is documented later (1899, 1908) |
| M5 | **"Jack" comes from the jack-knife** | Embellished | C009, C010, C001 | An 1881 engineer said the *jack-knife switch* was named for the knife shape it first had. But "spring-jack" is older (1877, telegraph), and the origin of the bare word "jack" was not found |
| M6 | **The 3.5 mm plug was designed in the 1950s for Japanese transistor radios** | Unverifiable | C017, C019 | No document dated before 1969 giving a 3.5 mm size was found. A 1959 Sony ad shows a radio sold with an earphone, but not the plug size. The 1969 IEC standard for 3.5 mm is titled "audio circuits in radio receivers", which supports the radio link, not the 1950s date |
| M7 | **The Sony EFM-117J (1964) popularised the 3.5 mm plug** | Unverifiable | — | No 1964 document was found (Smithsonian record behind a bot check; no Sony manual or ad found) |
| M8 | **The Walkman gave us the stereo mini plug** (a Japanese engineering society's citation) | Unverifiable | C020, C021, C022 | The society gives no record. A 1981 magazine shows pocket players using a 3.5 mm three-conductor plug, but not who first did |
| M9 | **Apple said "courage" when it dropped the jack** | Confirmed (secondary only) | C023, C024 | A live report of the keynote prints "Courage to move on". Apple's own press release does not contain the word, and Apple's keynote text/video was not obtained |
| M10 | **The headphone jack is dying, replaced by wireless** | Unverifiable | C025 | Only a forecast was found (448 million Bluetooth earbuds in 2024); no primary count of wired products or jack-equipped devices exists in what we could read |
