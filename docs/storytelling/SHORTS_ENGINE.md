# Shorts Engine: a whole story in 30–60 seconds (v1, 27 Oct 2026)

**AK's question (27 Oct 2026):** "Within 30–60 sec how can we tell a story that will have the same as long-form videos?"

**Answer in one line:** a Short keeps the long video's *arc* (question → stakes → turn → answer → ending) but cuts everything that isn't on the **causal chain**. It isn't a clip of a long video. It's the same story told with five moves instead of five chapters.

This file adds to STORY_ENGINE.md (the 3 engines, the T1–T16 toolbox, the 13 formats, audience profiles, story gates). Evidence IDs (E…) are in EVIDENCE.md, and E20–E25 were added for Shorts. **[craft]** marks storytelling craft; **[test]** marks our bets, to be checked against analytics; **[house]** marks AK's existing rules.

---

## 1. Why a full story fits in 30 seconds

- **A story's arc is a pattern, not a length.** Brewer & Lichtenstein's minimal *complete* suspense story is five sentences (E20): "The sniper was waiting outside the house. Charles got up from the chair. He walked slowly toward the window. There was the sound of a shot and the window broke. Charles fell dead." Initiating event, delay, outcome: the whole engine is there.
- **Viewers fill gaps themselves.** People work out "why actions, events, and states are mentioned" and supply the missing causal steps (E21). A Short can leave out every step a viewer can infer.
- **The picture can carry what the voice doesn't say.** Narration with matching animation beats on-screen text (E12: modality 1.17, temporal contiguity 1.30). So the *setting* goes to the eye and the *story* goes to the ear.

What does **not** compress: the question, the turn and the ending. Cut those and it stops being a story; it becomes a fact.

## 2. Long form vs Short: the same arc, mapped

| Long video (STORY_ENGINE section 6) | Short move | 30 s | 45 s | 60 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold open: the strongest question | **M1 Hook** | 0–2 s | 0–2 s | 0–3 s |
| Prime: names, parts, place | **M2 Ground** | 2–7 s | 2–9 s | 3–12 s |
| Chapters 1–3, each with a "but" | **M3 Push** (one "but" at 30–45 s; two at 60 s) | 7–18 s | 9–28 s | 12–38 s |
| Peak at about 2/3 | **M4 Turn** | 18–25 s | 28–38 s | 38–50 s |
| Click plus ending/callback | **M5 Echo** | 25–30 s | 38–45 s | 50–60 s |

**The five moves:**
1. **M1 Hook (open the gap or the stakes).** One image plus one line that makes a question. It pays the title's promise at once. Analytics are anchored on *engaged* views, meaning viewers who keep watching past the first moments (E25); the opening decides that. **[house]** Hook in 1–2 s.
2. **M2 Ground (prime).** Who, where and when in **one breath**, plus one thing the viewer already knows (T1: curiosity needs a knowledge base, E1). Show the setting in the picture; don't narrate it (C3).
3. **M3 Push (the "but").** The obstacle, clue or round. **Exactly one** "but" in 30–45 s, two at most in 60 s. Each line *causes* the next (C2). Foreshadow the turn here with a word (C5).
4. **M4 Turn (the peak).** The broken prediction, where the expectation flips (E5, E10). Put it late, about two-thirds through (T9).
5. **M5 Echo (click plus loop).** The answer in one insight (T2), with a last line that *rhymes* with M1, so the replay feels like a beginning **[house: loop ending]**. Resolve the loop you opened (E6).

## 3. Compression rules (C1–C13)

| # | Rule | Evidence | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | **One question, one character, one turn.** Everything else is a long-video chapter or a different Short | E1 (insight beats incremental), E6 | Can you say the question in under 10 words? |
| C2 | **Keep only the causal chain.** Viewers try to explain why each thing is mentioned, so anything that doesn't cause the next beat is noise they will try to explain | E21 | Read it with "but" / "so" between lines. Delete any line where "and then" fits |
| C3 | **The picture carries the setting; the voice carries the story.** Era, place and clothes go on screen; the narration never says "It was 1941 in Switzerland" if the picture can | E12 (modality, temporal contiguity) | Cover the screen: does the voice still tell the story? Mute it: does the picture still show where you are? |
| C4 | **Imply what a viewer can infer.** Skip steps the audience fills in themselves | E21 | Ask: would a 12-year-old notice the gap? If not, cut the step |
| C5 | **Foreshadow every jump with a few words** ("Ten years later…", "Then a mistake…") | E22 | No cut in time or place without a signpost |
| C6 | **Prime with what everyone knows.** Start from a shoe strap, a map, a price, never from zero | E1 | Is the first concrete image something the target viewer has seen? |
| C7 | **At most one number per move,** and give it a perspective | E13; STORY_FORMAT pacing | Count the numbers: 30 s ≤ 2, 45 s ≤ 3, 60 s ≤ 4 |
| C8 | **Cut words, never speed up the voice.** The word budget (section 4) is a ceiling | E23; AK's followable-pacing rule | Voice at speed 1. Pauses stay (0.3–0.6 s after key facts) |
| C9 | **The title's promise is paid or opened in M1** | E19, E25 | The first line and the title make the same promise without repeating each other (STORY_FORMAT double-barrel) |
| C10 | **Peak late, end resolved.** The strongest moment sits at about 2/3; the last line closes the loop | E6, E11 | Is M4 really the biggest moment? Is the question answered by the end? |
| C11 | **Plant and pay off inside the Short.** The turn must reinterpret something shown in M1 or M2 | E6 (surprise is fair when it re-explains), E5 | Point to the frame where the payoff was planted |
| C12 | **A new visual every 2–3 s, cut on story beats** | **[house]** Shorts formula; E10 (boundaries at prediction errors) | No shot longer than 3 s unless it is the M4 hold |
| C13 | **It must stand alone.** No "part 1", no "watch the full video to understand". A pointer to the long video comes *after* the loop closes, if at all | E6 (resolved stories are liked); YouTube treats Shorts as their own feed | Would it work for someone who never sees the long video? |

## 4. Word budgets (our voice, speed 1)

Measured pace: about 2.9–3.4 words per second (Indica Atlas Week 1, Week 2 scripts). Each move ends on a pause, and the end holds about 1 s for the loop.

| Length | Spoken words (ceiling) | Numbers | "But"s | Typical moves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **30 s** | 65–80 | ≤ 2 | 1 | M1, M2, M3, M4, M5, one or two sentences each |
| **45 s** | 100–120 | ≤ 3 | 1 | M3 can take two sentences |
| **60 s** | 135–160 | ≤ 4 | 2 | M3 splits into Push 1 and Push 2 |

**Over 60 s:** Shorts may run to 3 minutes (E24), but a Short over one minute with **any** copyright claim is blocked worldwide (E24). Over 60 s, use only YouTube Audio Library music or our own sound. AK's target is 30–60 s, which is what this file covers.

## 5. Choosing the length from the story

| The story has… | Length | Formats that fit |
|---|---|---|
| One surprising fact plus one click | **30 s** | F13 Scale Shock, F10 Paradox, F3 Happy Accident |
| One turn after one obstacle | **45 s** | F1 Myth Flip, F8 Then and Now, F9 Face-off, F4 Cold Case (one clue) |
| Two obstacles before the turn, or three zoom levels, or two quotes | **60 s** | F2 Long Road, F6 Zoom, F5 Own Words, F7 Follow the Thing (three stops) |
| More than two "buts" | Not a Short | Long video, or split into separate *complete* Shorts, each with its own question |

**Channel defaults** (from each channel's docs):
- **Inventor's Desk:** 45–60 s. STORY_FORMAT says about 90 s; AK's 27 Oct ask is 30–60 s, so align STORY_FORMAT on AK's word.
- **Data Kadai:** 30–45 s **[house]**.
- **Indica Atlas:** 25–35 s **[house]**.
- **Moat Atlas:** 30–50 s, captions only.

## 6. The 13 formats as Shorts

| Format | M1 Hook | M2 Ground | M3 Push | M4 Turn | M5 Echo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 Myth Flip | The belief, plus "the record says otherwise" | Who said what, when | The document | The real mechanism | The belief again, now hollow |
| F2 Long Road | The surprising failure | Who and why | Try → but (twice at 60 s) | The fix | The failure line, flipped |
| F3 Happy Accident | "X exists because of a mistake" | The plan | Why the plan was stuck | The slip that worked | The mistake, thanked |
| F4 Cold Case | The odd fact | Prime: what's normal | One clue (source on screen) | The document that solves it | Back to the odd fact, explained |
| F5 Own Words | Their line | Who, when | Line 2 | What they didn't know | Their line, re-heard |
| F6 Zoom | The everyday object | Level 1 | Level 2 | The one structure | Zoom out to the world |
| F7 Follow the Thing | "This ₹100 starts here" | Stop 1 | Stop 2 | The surprise stop | Where it ends |
| F8 Then and Now | "Guess the number in [year]" | The reveal | "Guess now" | The change | Every state, plus a question |
| F9 Face-off | Two contestants | Round 1 | Round 2 | The twist round | The data's verdict, plus "who would you pick?" |
| F10 Paradox | Fact A | Fact B (the clash) | "Both are true" | The hidden factor | Fact A, re-read |
| F11 Claim Check | The headline as a scene | The old dream | How it works | The catch, plus the ladder stamp | Callback question |
| F12 Quiet Turn | Introduce | Develop | (folded into M2) | The unexpected angle | Reconcile |
| F13 Scale Shock | The raw number | Perspective 1 | Perspective 2 | What it means for you | The number, now felt |

## 7. From a long video to Shorts: two honest ways

1. **Spine cut.** The whole long story's spine in five moves (section 2). Same research and claim IDs, new script. Don't trim the long script down; rewrite it to the word budget.
2. **Chapter cut.** One long-video chapter that has **its own question and turn**, rebuilt as a complete Short (M1–M5). Never a random clip that ends mid-thought (C13).

Moat Atlas plans "3 Shorts cut from the long video" (LAUNCH_PLAN). Under this rule, each of the three must be a spine cut or a chapter cut.

## 8. Shorts gate (on top of story gates S1–S13)

| Gate | Check |
|---|---|
| SH1 | The question is stated or implied by 2 s (Indica Atlas and Data Kadai) or 3 s (60 s Shorts) |
| SH2 | Word count is within the budget for the length (section 4); voice at speed 1 |
| SH3 | Exactly one "but" in 30–45 s, two at most in 60 s |
| SH4 | Every line passes the but/so test (C2) |
| SH5 | Setting carried by the picture, not narrated (C3) |
| SH6 | Every time or place jump has a signpost (C5) |
| SH7 | Numbers within the limit, each with a perspective (C7) |
| SH8 | The M4 turn is planted earlier (C11) |
| SH9 | The M5 line rhymes with M1 and the loop is closed (C10) |
| SH10 | It stands alone (C13) |
| SH11 | Over 60 s: no copyrighted audio (E24) |

## 9. Worked example: one research folder, three lengths (Velcro)

Files: `src/gadget/velcroglow/script_short30.json`, `script_short45.json`, `script_short60.json`. All pass `research_check.py --script`. (The research itself is still NOT CLEARED: G4 and G6 are open.)

**30 s · F3 Happy Accident · 68 words**

| Move | Line | Claims |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | Velcro exists because someone grabbed the wrong spool. | C040 |
| M2 | Swiss inventor George de Mestral wanted to copy the tiny hooks on a burr. | C001, C044, C045 |
| M3 | But hooks need a thread that bends, then holds its shape once heated. | C046 |
| M4 | He said a storeroom worker sent the wrong thread by mistake, and the one they ordered would never have worked. | C040 |
| M5 | One wrong spool. Next time you rip open a strap, thank a storeroom. | C040 |

**45 s · F1 Myth Flip · 105 words**

| Move | Line | Claims |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | Velcro is called a perfect copy of a burr. Its inventor's own patent says the first try barely worked. | C010, C014 |
| M2 | George de Mestral said the idea came in 1941, from burrs stuck in his dog's ears. | C021, C023, C024 |
| M3 | In 1951 he patented nylon hooks on both strips, hooks grabbing hooks. But his later patent admits only about thirty percent ever caught. | C002, C007, C010, C014 |
| M4 | So in 1957 he gave one side soft loops. About a thousand in every square centimetre, and far more hooks catch. | C013, C015 |
| M5 | And a 2015 study says Velcro works less like a burr than like a different plant. The perfect copy was never that simple. | C028 |

**60 s · F2 Long Road · 125 words** (deliberately under budget for calm pacing)

| Move | Line | Claims |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | Velcro's first design had no loops at all. Just hooks, grabbing other hooks. | C010 |
| M2 | Its inventor, George de Mestral, later said it began in 1941: burrs in his dog's ears, and under a microscope, hooks like tiny chamois horns. | C001, C021, C023, C024, C025, C044 |
| M3a | Ten years later he patented nylon hooks. Loop a thread over a hot bar, snip one side, and the loop becomes a hook. | C038, C002, C007, C008 |
| M3b | But with hooks on both strips, his own later patent says, only about thirty percent of them caught. | C010, C014 |
| M4 | So in 1957 he turned one side into soft loops. About a thousand in every square centimetre, and far more hooks find something to grab. | C013, C015 |
| M5 | He called the invention five percent of the work. The rest was sweat. Hooks that missed, until they didn't. | C042, C014 |

**What each length dropped, and why:**
- **The 30 s** drops the patents, the loops and space. It keeps one question ("why the wrong spool?") and one turn (C1).
- **The 45 s** drops the storeroom and the 1963 test (one "but" only, SH3).
- **The 60 s** drops the 2015 twist and space, to keep two clean "buts" and the strongest end line.

The dropped material isn't wasted: each piece is the seed of its own complete Short. For example, "In 1963, people walked on an aircraft ceiling with Velcro" is a 30 s F13/F3.
