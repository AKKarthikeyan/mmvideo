# Story Engine: how research becomes a story (v1, 27 Oct 2026)

**What this is.** The second half of our system.
- RESEARCH_RULES decides **what is true**.
- The Story Engine decides **how to tell it** so a real audience stays, understands and remembers.

It serves all four channels: The Inventor's Desk, Data Kadai, Indica Atlas and Moat Atlas. It is written to be the brief for a story-writing AI agent, word for word.

**Input:** a research folder whose gates passed, or that is clearly marked as a draft.
**Output:** a script in which every factual line carries claim IDs.

**Built from evidence, not habit.** Every rule below cites a finding in EVIDENCE.md (E1–E19), each read at its original source. Rules from storytelling craft traditions are marked **[craft]**. Rules that are our own bets, to be tested with analytics, are marked **[test]**.

**Rule zero: the story serves the facts.** A format never adds, bends or upgrades a fact. If the best format needs a fact the research doesn't have, choose another format or send the gap back to research. Hedges from the ledger stay in the voice ("he later said", "records suggest").

---

## 1. The three engines (what makes any story pull)

Brewer & Lichtenstein (E6) showed that stories entertain through three discourse structures, and that people like resolved ones best. Every format below runs on one main engine.

| Engine | How it is built | What the viewer feels | Our rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity** | Show that something important is *missing*, then supply it later | "Wait, how? Why?" | The viewer must *know* a piece is missing (E6). Give a little context first (E1), then point at the hole |
| **Suspense** | Show an event with stakes early, put *relevant* material between it and the outcome, then give the outcome | "Will it work?" | Stakes must be clear; the delay must be content, not padding (E6, E12 weeding) |
| **Surprise** | Hold back a key fact *without* saying so, then reveal it so everything reinterprets | "Oh! So that's why." | Fair play: the reveal must re-explain what was already shown. Plant it earlier **[craft]** (E5: learning comes from the gap between expected and received) |

**Always resolve.** Every loop opened is closed in the same video, or explicitly handed to a named next episode (E6: resolved structures were liked better).

## 2. The toolbox: 16 rules every format uses

| # | Rule | Why (evidence) | How it looks |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | **Prime, then gap.** Give one or two things the viewer already knows before opening the question | Curiosity needs a knowledge base (E1, p.89) | Indica Atlas: show where the island is on the globe *before* asking why it's closer to the Maldives |
| T2 | **One question, one click.** The answer should be one insight that closes the whole gap, not a list | Curiosity is stronger for insight than for incremental problems (E1, p.88) | Velcro: "two hook layers, only 30% caught" is the click that explains the loops |
| T3 | **Ask them to predict** before the reveal, then pause | Predicting raises curiosity and memory (E4) | "Guess which state is first." (1 s hold) |
| T4 | **Say the expectation out loud, then beat it** | Learning follows the gap between expected and received (E5); curiosity helps memory of surprising answers (E2) | "You'd think the burr was the blueprint. A 2015 study says it isn't." |
| T5 | **Ride the curiosity.** Put the key teaching *inside* the curious stretch, not after it | Curious states also boost memory for what is shown alongside (E3) | The mechanism goes between the question and the answer |
| T6 | **Cut at broken predictions.** Beat and chapter boundaries go where an expectation breaks, nested in levels | Event boundaries are felt at prediction errors and organise memory hierarchically (E10) | Short: 3 mini-events inside one arc. Long: chapters of 3–5 beats, each ending on a turn |
| T7 | **Predictable form, unpredictable content.** Use the same clear signposts ("First…", "But…", "So…") so viewers can anticipate *how* while wondering *what* | Understanding tracks how well listeners anticipate the teller (E9) | Every Inventor's Desk episode uses the same beat names |
| T8 | **Plan the feeling curve.** Give each beat an emotion (wonder, worry, relief, delight, pride) | Engagement is shared across viewers and driven by emotion; engaged moments are remembered (E8) | Mark the emotion in the beat sheet; avoid three flat beats in a row |
| T9 | **Peak and end.** Put the strongest moment late, about two-thirds through, and end on a resolved, meaningful line plus a callback, never on admin | Experiences are remembered by peak and end, not length (E11; applying it to video is our extrapolation) | Velcro's peak: 1,000 loops and almost every hook catches. End: "5% invention, the rest sweat" |
| T10 | **Words to the ear, keywords to the eye** | Narration beats on-screen text (1.17); identical narration and text hurts (0.69); keep words in sync with pictures (1.30); words near the graphic (0.48) (E12) | On-screen text: the number, the name, three words at most. Never the full sentence the voice is saying. Exception: captions-only formats (Moat Atlas Shorts) |
| T11 | **Name the parts first** | Pretraining on names and parts (1.00) (E12) | "A hook. A loop. Now watch them meet." Then the mechanism |
| T12 | **Weed and signal.** Cut interesting-but-irrelevant detail; point at what matters | Weeding (0.90), signalling (0.74) (E12) | One highlight colour per frame; no trivia detours, however charming |
| T13 | **Segment with breath.** Pause after each key fact | Segmenting (1.36) (E12); matches AK's pacing rule | 0.4–0.6 s hold after every fact (STORY_FORMAT pacing) |
| T14 | **Music under, not over** | Background music adds processing load during explanation (E12, p.45) | Duck music under dense explanation; let it rise at the peak and the end |
| T15 | **Give every number a perspective** | Perspective sentences improve recall and estimation (E13) | "₹6.3 lakh crore: about ₹4,500 for every Indian." "1,000 loops in a fingernail-sized square." |
| T16 | **Pay the promise fast** | YouTube's Intro metric is the share still watching after 30 s and reflects whether the opening matches the title and thumbnail (E19) | The title's promise is paid or clearly opened in the first 2 s of a Short or 30 s of a long video |

**Three things we don't do:**
- **No guilt lever.** "One named face beats statistics" failed replication (E17). Use one character for *clarity and imagery* (E7), never to shame or pressure.
- **No spoiler theory.** The evidence is mixed (E18). Lead with an outcome only when it creates a *new* question (the Myth Flip, for example), not because "spoilers help".
- **No persuasion by story.** Absorbed viewers notice fewer errors (E7), and narrative is persuasive (E15). That makes accuracy *our* job, not the viewer's. The research gates exist for this reason.

## 3. The format library (13 formats)

Each format lists:
- the **promise** it makes to the viewer and the **engine** it runs on;
- what the **research must contain** for it to work;
- its **beats** (Short / Long);
- which **channels** it suits;
- the **trap** to avoid.

Shorts: 25–60 s (Inventor's Desk up to ~90 s). Long: 8–12 min unless the channel says otherwise.

### F1 · The Myth Flip: "What everyone gets wrong"
- **Engine:** surprise, then curiosity.
- **Needs:** a `myths.md` item marked *Contradicted* or *Embellished*, backed by primary evidence; the myth must be widely held (T1).
- **Short beats:**
  1. The belief, stated plainly.
  2. "But the record says…"
  3. Evidence 1 (show the document).
  4. Evidence 2.
  5. The real story in one click (T2).
  6. Why the myth grew (how the telling drifted, RESEARCH_RULES 2.3).
  7. A callback question.
- **Long:** cold open on the myth → three chapters, one per piece of evidence, each ending on a turn → the real story → how the myth spread → what it changes.
- **Channels:** all. Data Kadai's Myth-buster type; Moat Atlas "what the filing actually says".
- **Trap:** never mock the people who believed it. Never flip a myth on secondary-only evidence.

### F2 · The Long Road: "Try, but, so"
- **Engine:** suspense.
- **Needs:** at least two dated attempts that fell short before success, each verified.
- **Short beats:**
  1. Hook: the surprising failure.
  2. The need.
  3. Try 1, BUT…
  4. SO try 2, BUT…
  5. SO the fix (the peak).
  6. Today.
  7. Callback.
- **Long:** each try is a chapter; the midpoint is the worst failure; the last chapter is the fix and its cost.
- **Channels:** Inventor's Desk (its seven beats in STORY_FORMAT.md *are* this format), Moat Atlas company histories, Indica Atlas explorers.
- **Trap:** a list of dates isn't a road. Every step must *cause* the next ("but… so…", STORY_FORMAT).

### F3 · The Happy Accident
- **Engine:** surprise.
- **Needs:** a verified accident, mistake or chance event, ideally in the person's own words, plus the mechanism that explains why the accident worked.
- **Short beats:**
  1. The plan.
  2. The slip.
  3. Nobody knew yet…
  4. Why the slip worked (the mechanism, T5).
  5. What it led to.
  6. Callback.
- **Long:** three accidents, or one accident with the hunt for why it worked.
- **Channels:** Inventor's Desk (the "Happy Accidents" series), Indica Atlas (accidents of geography).
- **Trap:** don't turn years of deliberate work into luck. The research must show the accident happened, and the script must say how much was work.

### F4 · The Cold Case: "Let's investigate"
- **Engine:** curiosity.
- **Needs:** a real puzzle the viewer can state, clues found in named sources, and a document that settles it.
- **Short beats:**
  1. The odd fact or object.
  2. "Why?" (the gap, stated).
  3. Clue 1, with the source on screen.
  4. A tempting wrong answer.
  5. Clue 2.
  6. The document that solves it.
  7. What it means.
- **Long:** the research journey *is* the plot: archive, patent, film, map. Show the hunt.
- **Channels:** all. It suits us especially, because our research is real and showing it builds trust (Data Kadai's "Can I trust this?" need).
- **Trap:** a fake mystery the answer doesn't earn. If the answer is dull, use another format.

### F5 · In Their Own Words
- **Engine:** curiosity plus transportation (E7).
- **Needs:** three or more verified P1 quotes from the person (recordings, letters, interviews), with dates. Translations labelled.
- **Short beats:**
  1. Their most striking line.
  2. Who said it, and when.
  3. Line 2, explained.
  4. Line 3, the turn.
  5. What we know now that they didn't.
  6. Their line again, with new meaning.
- **Long:** structure the film around 5–7 quotes; narration only bridges.
- **Channels:** Inventor's Desk, Indica Atlas (explorers' reports), Moat Atlas (shareholder letters, management's own words labelled).
- **Trap:** never put words in their mouth; never shorten a quote so that its meaning changes. Also usable as a **layer** inside any format.

### F6 · The Zoom: "From your hand to the hook"
- **Engine:** curiosity (insight).
- **Needs:** a verified mechanism with three or more nested scales.
- **Short beats:**
  1. An everyday object.
  2. Zoom level 1.
  3. Level 2.
  4. The one structure that explains it (the click).
  5. Zoom out to world scale.
- **Long:** zoom chapters with one question per level.
- **Channels:** Inventor's Desk (the Desk Glow Dive camera), Indica Atlas (globe → region → town), Moat Atlas (company → segment → product → unit economics).
- **Trap:** pretty zooms without a question at each level. Name the parts first (T11).

### F7 · Follow the Thing: "Where does it go?"
- **Engine:** curiosity, with mild suspense.
- **Needs:** a traceable path through several hands (₹, $, a sweater, a gene, a data packet), with each stop sourced.
- **Short beats:**
  1. "This ₹100 starts here."
  2. Stop 1, one number.
  3. Stop 2.
  4. The surprise stop (who keeps the most).
  5. Where it ends.
  6. Question.
- **Long:** Moat Atlas's *Follow the Dollar*: one Sankey-like journey, one chapter per hand.
- **Channels:** Moat Atlas (flagship), Data Kadai (Money and Economy days), Indica Atlas (trade routes; Panipat's used woollens).
- **Trap:** every stop's number must come from the same period and basis (RESEARCH_RULES 4.5).

### F8 · Then and Now: "Look how far it moved"
- **Engine:** surprise through change.
- **Needs:** the same measure at two or more dates, with the same definition.
- **Short beats:**
  1. Predict *then* (T3).
  2. Reveal.
  3. Predict *now*.
  4. Reveal the change.
  5. Why (only if a source states it).
  6. Every state on the map.
- **Channels:** Data Kadai (Change type), Indica Atlas (maps through time), Moat Atlas (five-year trends).
- **Trap:** a change across a definition change is not a change (RESEARCH_RULES 2.5).

### F9 · Face-off: "Two paths"
- **Engine:** suspense (who wins?).
- **Needs:** two comparable subjects on the same basis.
- **Short beats:**
  1. Meet the two.
  2. Round 1.
  3. Round 2.
  4. The twist round, where the underdog wins one.
  5. The data's verdict (not ours).
  6. "Who would you pick?"
- **Long:** rounds as chapters; peer drawn to the same scale (Moat Atlas rule).
- **Channels:** Data Kadai (Face-off, Kadai Wars), Moat Atlas (peers), Indica Atlas (two cities, two rivers).
- **Trap:** no winner on a thin lead; no mocking the loser (Data Kadai's no-shaming rule).

### F10 · The Paradox: "Both are true. How?"
- **Engine:** curiosity.
- **Needs:** two verified facts that seem to clash, plus the verified hidden factor that reconciles them.
- **Short beats:**
  1. Fact A.
  2. Fact B, the clash.
  3. "Both are true."
  4. The hidden factor.
  5. Click.
  6. Callback.
- **Channels:** Indica Atlas ("Why" Shorts), Data Kadai (Paradox type), Inventor's Desk ("a fridge doesn't make cold").
- **Trap:** the hidden factor must be in the research, not guessed.

### F11 · The Claim Check: "How true is this headline?"
- **Engine:** curiosity, plus a credibility payoff.
- **Needs:** P5 sources (the paper, the filing, the regulator) and the claim ladder rung.
- **Beats:** as STORY_FORMAT's Now episodes:
  1. The headline as a scene.
  2. The old dream.
  3. The road to it.
  4. How it works.
  5. The catch.
  6. The ladder stamp.
  7. Callback.
- **Channels:** Inventor's Desk (Now), Moat Atlas (earnings claims vs filings), Data Kadai ("What is X, in numbers").
- **Trap:** speed must never skip a gate.

### F12 · The Quiet Turn (four-part, no villain) [craft]
- **Engine:** surprise, gently resolved.
- **Needs:** a wonder topic with no conflict: a landscape, a natural process, a craft.
- **Beats:** the craft tradition is the Japanese *kishōtenketsu* (introduce, develop, turn, reconcile):
  1. Introduce the thing.
  2. Develop it (how it lives or works).
  3. The turn: an unexpected angle that seems unrelated.
  4. Reconcile: how the turn and the thing fit together.

  Evidence link: surprise plus resolution (E6).
- **Channels:** Indica Atlas (wonder Shorts), Inventor's Desk (calm "how it works"); also suits the Gita channels.
- **Trap:** a turn that never reconnects. The reconcile beat must make the turn click.

### F13 · Scale Shock: "One number, made human"
- **Engine:** surprise.
- **Needs:** one striking, verified number, plus two or three perspective comparisons computed from sources (E13).
- **Short beats:**
  1. The raw number (too big to feel).
  2. Perspective 1 (ratio: per person, per day).
  3. Perspective 2 (rank or unit change).
  4. What it means for *you*.
  5. Source and date.
- **Channels:** Data Kadai (Outlier type), Moat Atlas Shorts, Inventor's Desk "Today" beats.
- **Trap:** the perspective must use the same year and basis, computed by script, never by hand.

## 4. Choosing a format from the research

Read `dossier.md`, `myths.md` and `claims.jsonl`, and pick the **main format** from the strongest signal. You may add **one layer**: F5 (own words) or F13 (scale).

| Signal in the research | Format |
|---|---|
| A *Contradicted* myth with P1 evidence | F1 Myth Flip |
| Two or more dated failures before success | F2 Long Road |
| A verified accident or mistake | F3 Happy Accident |
| A puzzle settled by a specific document | F4 Cold Case |
| Three or more verified quotes from the person | F5 Own Words (or as a layer) |
| A mechanism with three or more nested scales | F6 Zoom |
| A traceable chain of hands or money | F7 Follow the Thing |
| Same measure at two or more dates | F8 Then and Now |
| Two comparable rivals | F9 Face-off |
| Two true facts that clash | F10 Paradox |
| A fresh claim in the news | F11 Claim Check |
| A wonder with no conflict | F12 Quiet Turn |
| One number too big to feel | F13 Scale Shock |

**Rotation:** never use the same main format twice in a row on one channel. YouTube does not pay out on template videos "with little variation from video to video" (Moat Atlas LAUNCH_PLAN, section 3). **[test]** Rotation also keeps returning viewers guessing (E5).

## 5. Audience profiles: who we are talking to

Each channel's audience changes the length, the words, the hook type and which formats rank highest. Facts about each audience come from that channel's own docs; replace them with the channel's analytics as they arrive (section 7).

### The Inventor's Desk
- **Who:** global; a curious 12-year-old and the parent beside them; general audience, not "made for kids" (STORY_FORMAT).
- **Context:** Shorts feed, often watched together. Up to ~90 s.
- **Language:** plain English, at most one technical word, explained in the same breath (T11); metric units with worldwide comparisons.
- **Best formats:** F2, F3, F6, F1, F5 (layer), F12, F11 for Now episodes.
- **Hooks:** a strange scene, never a date (STORY_FORMAT); a prediction question (T3).
- **Avoid:** fear for its own sake, gore, babyish lines, cast lists.
- **Ending:** a callback, plus a comment question a child can answer.

### Data Kadai
- **Who:** Indian viewers, with five needs: my pocket, explain the moment, things I use, my state, can I trust this (AUDIENCE.md). Hindi and Tamil preference is high (AUDIENCE.md).
- **Context:** Shorts 30–45 s, two a day; long-form 20–25 min, often on TV screens (AUDIENCE.md).
- **Language:** simple English with regional audio tracks; lakh and crore where PREFERENCES says.
- **Best formats:** F8, F9, F13, F10, F1, F7 (₹ journeys), F4 ("Guess the state" is T3 inside F4).
- **Hooks:** the number lands in the first 3 s (AUDIENCE.md); a prediction of "your state".
- **Avoid:** rage-bait, mocking a state, politics, causes the source doesn't state (WRITER_GUIDE).
- **Ending:** every state on the map, then one question.

### Indica Atlas
- **Who:** about 50% Indian and 50% international viewers (AK's stated target when naming the channel, 20 Oct 2026). The international half has little prior knowledge of the place.
- **Context:** "Why" Shorts of 25–35 s; long videos of 8–12 min.
- **Language:** English for a global ear; place names said carefully (research claim on pronunciation).
- **Best formats:** F10, F6, F4, F12, F7, F5 (explorers), F1.
- **Hooks:** a comparison the whole world can picture ("three times closer to the Maldives than to Kerala").
- **Must:** prime with the globe first (T1); the official map; neutral wording on borders and communities (RESEARCH_RULES 4.4).
- **Avoid:** exoticising, "mysterious India" clichés, side-taking.

### Moat Atlas
- **Who:** English-speaking investors in the US, UK and Europe (LAUNCH_PLAN).
- **Context:** long videos of 8–12 min (earnings) and 15–20 min (Follow the Dollar); Shorts of 30–50 s, **captions only, no narration** (house format).
- **Language:** sober, precise; calendar quarters; currency stated.
- **Best formats:** F7, F9, F4, F1 (vs the filing), F13, F11, F2 (company history, researched under the Inventor's Desk rules).
- **Hooks:** a number from the filing that contradicts the narrative.
- **Captions-only Shorts:** on-screen text *is* the narration, so T10 flips. Keep each caption to one idea and segment it (T13).
- **Avoid:** advice, hype adjectives, an AI persona posing as a human expert (LAUNCH_PLAN, section 3).

## 6. Beat grids

**Short (35–45 s, ~95–120 words):**

| Time | Beat | Engine work |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 s | Hook: the promise | Open the gap or the stakes (T16) |
| 2–8 s | Prime | One known thing (T1) |
| 8–20 s | Build | Clue, try or round, with a prediction prompt (T3) |
| 20–30 s | Turn | The broken prediction (T6) and the peak (T9) |
| 30–40 s | Click | One insight (T2), with perspective (T15) |
| 40–45 s | End | Callback plus question; loop to the start |

Inventor's Desk 90 s Shorts stretch this to the seven STORY_FORMAT beats.

**Long (8–12 min):**

| Time | Section | Engine work |
|---|---|---|
| 0–30 s | Cold open | The strongest *question* (not the answer), plus the promise of the video (T16) |
| 0:30–1:30 | Prime | Names, parts and places (T11); why it matters |
| Chapters 1–3 (2–3 min each) | Build | Each chapter opens a small loop and closes it on a turn that opens the next (T6) |
| ~2/3 mark | Peak | The biggest reveal or reversal (T9) |
| Final chapter | Click | The main question answered in one insight (T2) |
| Last 30–45 s | End | A meaningful line and a callback to the cold open. Admin goes in the description, not the ending (T9) |

## 7. Measure and learn [test]

For every published video, log:
- the main format and the hook type;
- the YouTube Intro figure (share still watching at 30 s) (E19);
- the timestamps of dips and spikes, mapped to beat IDs.

After about ten videos per format on a channel, compare the formats:
- **A dip at the same beat type** in several videos means the rule behind that beat is failing for this audience. Rewrite the rule here and date the change.
- **A spike** can mean "loved" or "unclear" (E19). Read the comments before deciding.

Data Kadai's Monday listening loop (AUDIENCE.md section 4) is the model.

## 8. Story gate: all must pass before voicing

| Gate | Check |
|---|---|
| S1 Facts | Every factual line carries claim IDs, and its status allows narration (`research_check.py --script`) |
| S2 Format | The main format is named, with the research signal that chose it (section 4) |
| S3 Question | One question is stated or implied by 6 s (Short) or 30 s (long) |
| S4 Prime | The question is preceded by something the viewer knows (T1) |
| S5 Resolve | Every loop opened is closed (E6) |
| S6 Peak | The peak is marked, late in the video (T9) |
| S7 End | The last line is a callback or meaningful line, not admin |
| S8 Screen text | Keywords and numbers only (T10), except captions-only formats |
| S9 Numbers | Every number has a perspective or a reason to be there (T15); at most one number per beat (STORY_FORMAT pacing) |
| S10 Hedges | Ledger hedges are kept word for word |
| S11 Retell | A viewer in the target audience could retell it in three sentences (STORY_FORMAT) |
| S12 Audience | Nothing on the channel profile's "avoid" list (section 5) |
| S13 Review | One pass with the Bhagyaraj skill (heart, plants and payoffs) and one with the Nolan skill (structure, the turn), noted in the script file |

## 9. Worked example: one research, five stories (Velcro)

All from `docs/inventors_desk/research/P01-velcro`. Same facts, different engines.

| Format | Hook (first line) | Spine |
|---|---|---|
| **F2 Long Road** (script_v3) | "Velcro's first design had no loops at all. Just hooks, grabbing other hooks." [C010] | The burrs (his words) → idea sleeps 10 years → patent 1951 → only 30% caught → loops 1957 → 2015 twist → 1963 ceiling walk → "5% invention" |
| **F1 Myth Flip** | "Everyone says Velcro copied a burr perfectly. Its inventor's own patent says the first try barely worked." [C010, C014, C028] | The myth (one strip of hooks, one of loops, copied from nature) → patent shows hooks on hooks → 30% → loops added in 1957 → the 2015 study → how the tidy story grew |
| **F3 Happy Accident** | "Velcro exists because someone grabbed the wrong spool." [C040] | The plan in Lyon → the storeroom mistake (his words) → why that thread worked (nylon that holds its shape when heated, C046/C047) → the first sample in three weeks [C039] → callback |
| **F5 Own Words** | "'Like chamois horns. A perfection.' That's how Velcro's inventor described what he saw." [C044] | Chamois horns → "if I can make hooks like these, I'll have made a fastener" [C045] → "an invention is 5%… 95% sweat" [C042] → "I didn't make a fortune" [C049] |
| **F6 Zoom** | "Your shoe strap holds about a thousand loops in a square the size of a fingernail." [C015; perspective needs a sourced fingernail size, so check first] | Shoe → strip → hooks and loops → one hook catching one loop → astronauts on a 1963 aircraft ceiling [C030] |

Note on F6: the "fingernail" perspective is only an example. A perspective is itself a fact and needs a source before use (T15, S9).
