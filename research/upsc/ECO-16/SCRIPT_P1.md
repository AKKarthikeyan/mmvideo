## Chapter 2

### b02 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1 · new term: double coincidence of wants
**PICTURE:** A shoe-maker and a wheat farmer face each other in an atlas-style market; the farmer shakes his head and points to a plough; a thought bubble of shoes crossed out. Then a coin appears between them and the line between them closes.  
**VOICE:** Start with a world without money. NCERT's example is a shoe-maker who wants wheat. Barter works only if the wheat farmer also wants to buy shoes. Economists call this a double coincidence of wants. What one person wants to sell must be exactly what the other wants to buy. That is hard to find. So the trade needs a go-between that both sides accept. That go-between is called money.  
**SCREEN:** barter: needs a double coincidence of wants · money = a commonly accepted go-between · NCERT  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N01 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-N29 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** First small win, under 1:30: why do we need money at all? (STORYTELLING 3.7).

### b03 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2 · new term: medium of exchange
**PICTURE:** The shoe-maker hands over shoes, receives a coin; hands the coin to the farmer, receives wheat. The coin glows and travels along; a label slides in: MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE.  
**VOICE:** Money does three jobs. The first is to be a medium of exchange. The shoe-maker sells his shoes for money. Then he buys wheat with that money. Neither needs to want what the other makes.  
**SCREEN:** job 1: medium of exchange  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N01 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-N02 (CONFIRMED)

### b04 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2 · new term: unit of account
**PICTURE:** A stationery shelf: a pen with a tag ₹10, a pencil with a tag ₹2; five pencils line up beside the pen on a balance. A label slides in: UNIT OF ACCOUNT.  
**VOICE:** The second job is to be a unit of account. Every price is stated in rupees. NCERT's example: a pen costs ten rupees and a pencil costs two. So one pen is worth five pencils. Prices in rupees let us compare any two goods.  
**SCREEN:** job 2: unit of account · NCERT: pen ₹10, pencil ₹2 → 1 pen = 5 pencils  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N02 (CONFIRMED)

### b05 · *BUT* · rung 3 · new term: store of value
**PICTURE:** A jar of coins on a shelf; a shopping basket beside it shrinks year by year as price tags climb. Gold bar, a plot of land and a bond certificate stand to one side with a small padlock: SLOWER TO SPEND.  
**VOICE:** The third job is to be a store of value. A rupee kept today should buy about as much later. But when prices rise, its purchasing power falls. That is what a rupee can buy. NCERT says that, to store value well, the value of money must be sufficiently stable. Gold, land and bonds can store value too. But they may not be easily converted, and they are not accepted everywhere.  
**SCREEN:** job 3: store of value · prices rise → purchasing power falls · gold, land, bonds: may be harder to convert  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N03 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 3

### b06 · *BUT* · rung 3 · new term: demand for money
**PICTURE:** Two sliders on a desk. Slide INCOME up: a wallet grows fatter. Slide INTEREST RATE up: coins hop out of the wallet into a bank passbook. A label: DEMAND FOR MONEY.  
**VOICE:** So people want money. But money in a pocket earns nothing. How much of it do people hold? NCERT gives two forces. The more you spend, the more money you need at hand. So the demand for money rises with income. And when the interest rate rises, people hold less, because money in hand gives up that interest.  
**SCREEN:** demand for money: rises with income · falls when the interest rate rises (NCERT 3.2.1)  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N05 (CONFIRMED)

### b07 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: liquidity preference
**PICTURE:** Three jars on a table: SHOPPING (a basket), BAD DAY (an umbrella), WAITING (a bond certificate and a clock). One portrait plaque: KEYNES, 1936. A bracket over all three: LIQUIDITY PREFERENCE.  
**VOICE:** In 1936, Keynes split this demand into three motives. The transactions motive: money for the week's shopping and bills. The precautionary motive: money kept for a bad day. The speculative motive: money held to profit from judging the future of interest rates better than the market. Keynes called the whole thing liquidity preference, and NCERT uses that name too.  
**SCREEN:** Keynes (1936): 3 motives · transactions · precautionary · speculative → liquidity preference · jars: our picture  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-T14 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-N20 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Syllabus item (Keynes' theory on demand for money). The jars are our own picture, labelled as such on screen.

### b08 · *THEREFORE* · rung 4
**PICTURE:** A bond certificate on a seesaw against a rate dial. Dial up: the certificate sinks, a price tag ticks from 109.29 to 107.33. A figure with a calculator sells it and tucks the money in a wallet.  
**VOICE:** The speculative motive needs one fact. A bond's price falls when interest rates rise. NCERT's worked bond is worth about one hundred and nine rupees at five percent. At six percent, it is worth only about one hundred and seven. When rates are very high, people expect them to fall. They buy bonds and hold little money. When rates are low, more people expect them to rise. They sell bonds and hold money. So speculative demand for money falls as the interest rate rises.  
**SCREEN:** bond price falls as the interest rate rises · NCERT's bond: ₹109.29 at 5%, ₹107.33 at 6%  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N21 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Rung 4: NCERT Box 3.1 worked example; kept short because it is optional-paper depth.

### b09 · *BUT* · rung 4 · new term: liquidity trap
**PICTURE:** A tank of water labelled INTEREST RATE, the water at the floor. A pump pours money in; it pools in wallets and the water level does not move. A label: LIQUIDITY TRAP.  
**VOICE:** There is a limit to this. Suppose the interest rate is already so low that everyone expects it to rise. Then people simply hold on to money. Hand them more, and it just sits with them. The rate does not fall any further. NCERT calls this a liquidity trap.  
**SCREEN:** liquidity trap: rate very low, everyone expects it to rise → extra money is just held · NCERT Box 3.1  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N22 (CONFIRMED)

### b10 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: velocity of circulation
**PICTURE:** One note travels hand to hand along a street: fruit seller, milkman, bus conductor; a counter ticks 1, 2, 3 and a small tag says OUR PICTURE. A formula card slides in: v = 1/k.  
**VOICE:** There is an older way to see the same idea. Ask how often one rupee is used. Here is a picture, our own. You pay a hundred-rupee note to the fruit seller. She pays it to the milkman. He pays it to the bus conductor. The number of times a unit of money changes hands in a period is called its velocity of circulation. NCERT writes the demand for money for payments as k times the value of transactions. It says velocity is one divided by k.  
**SCREEN:** velocity of circulation = times a unit of money changes hands · NCERT: MdT = k·T · v = 1/k · picture: our own  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N20 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Analogy labelled as ours; it stops being true where a note sits idle (velocity is an average).

### b11 · *THEREFORE* · rung 4 · new term: equation of exchange
**PICTURE:** A balance scale: MONEY × SPEED on one pan, PRICES × TRADE on the other; both sides stay level as the left side doubles and the price tags double. A portrait plaque: FISHER, 1911. A small flag: ONLY IF TRADE AND SPEED STAY FIXED.  
**VOICE:** In 1911, Irving Fisher linked the money in circulation, bank deposits, the speed of each, and the volume of trade. IGNOU gives a simple form: money times its velocity equals the price level times the number of transactions. That is the equation of exchange. IGNOU's notes say that if trade is fixed and velocity steady, the price level follows the quantity of money. So in this classical quantity theory, double the money and prices double. If either assumption fails, the price level is unlikely to move in step with the money.  
**SCREEN:** Fisher (1911): equation of exchange · simple form (IGNOU): M·V = P·T · T fixed, V steady → P follows M  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-T10 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-T11 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-A13 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Syllabus item: Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher). The deeper inflation side belongs to ECO-15.

### b12 · *THEREFORE* · rung 4 · new term: Cambridge k
**PICTURE:** A wallet beside a salary slip with a dial: k = 1/4 of income kept as cash. Four hands pass the note along: velocity. The dial shrinks as the interest-rate arrow climbs.  
**VOICE:** Economists at Cambridge looked from the holder's side. Marshall, Pigou, Robertson and Keynes asked what share of income people choose to keep as money. They called that share k. It is the reciprocal of velocity. In 1950, Pigou wrote that it is a reasonable approximation to say this share falls when the interest rate rises. That is the demand for money we met earlier.  
**SCREEN:** Cambridge k = share of income held as money = 1/V · Pigou (1950): the share falls as the interest rate rises  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-T12 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-T13 (CONFIRMED)

### b13 · *THEREFORE* · rung 4
**PICTURE:** A shelf of assets in a row: cash, bond, share, house, a tool kit; a hand chooses among them by weighing each. A label: MONEY IS ONE ASSET. A plaque: FRIEDMAN.  
**VOICE:** Later, Milton Friedman restated it as a theory of the demand for money. IGNOU's notes say that for him, money is one asset among others. Bonds, shares, physical goods and human wealth are the others. He dropped Keynes's separate motives. Monetarists, who follow him, hold that velocity is fairly steady.  
**SCREEN:** Friedman: money = one asset among others · no separate motives · monetarists: velocity fairly steady · per IGNOU  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-T15 (CONFIRMED)

### b15 · *BUT* · rung 3 · new term: fiat money
**PICTURE:** A note close up, the promise clause glowing; the glow follows a hand-off from person to person and back to an RBI building. A tag: FIAT MONEY.  
**VOICE:** Why do we accept a piece of paper? It has no value of its own. Its value comes from the issuer's guarantee. NCERT calls this fiat money. Each note says, I promise to pay the bearer. Those words record the Reserve Bank's own obligation to whoever holds the note.  
**SCREEN:** fiat money: value from the issuer's guarantee · 'I promise to pay the bearer' = RBI's obligation (RBI Act s.26)  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N24 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-R12 (CONFIRMED)

### b16 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: legal tender
**PICTURE:** A shopkeeper tries to push a note back; a gentle red hand-stop appears over his palm; a label: LEGAL TENDER. A small question mark floats at the edge (plant: the cheque).  
**VOICE:** Section twenty-six goes further. Every banknote still in circulation is legal tender anywhere in India, guaranteed by the Central Government. Legal tender means it cannot be refused when it is offered in payment. Remember that line. It will matter in a minute.  
**SCREEN:** legal tender: cannot be refused · every banknote in circulation, anywhere in India (RBI Act s.26)  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-R11 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-N24 (CONFIRMED)

## Chapter 5

### b17 · *BUT* · rung 3 · new term: demand deposit
**PICTURE:** A customer writes a cheque; the money rises out of one account bar into another; the shopkeeper nods but his hand can wave it away. A badge flips: DEPOSIT = MONEY, NOT LEGAL TENDER.  
**VOICE:** But notes are not the whole story. Money in a bank account that you can take out on demand is money too. NCERT's Class 10 book says modern money is currency plus deposits. Why? You can pay with it by cheque, a written order to the bank. Such deposits are called demand deposits because they are payable on demand. Here is the catch. A cheque can be refused. So a demand deposit is money, but it is not legal tender.  
**SCREEN:** modern money = currency + deposits · demand deposits: withdraw any time, pay by cheque · not legal tender  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N30 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-N31 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-N24 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Pays the plant of b12 and the misconception 'a cheque is money like a note' (dossier A2).

### b18 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: broad money
**PICTURE:** M1 as a base block (cash + demand deposits) with two branches rising from it: M2 (adds post office savings) and M3 (adds time deposits); M4 sits above M3 (adds post office deposits). A bracket over M1-M2: NARROW. Over M3-M4: BROAD. M3 glows.  
**VOICE:** How do we count it all? NCERT lists four measures of money that the Reserve Bank has published. M1 is cash with the public plus demand deposits. M3 adds time deposits, such as fixed deposits, which have a fixed period to maturity. M2 and M4 add post office deposits. M1 and M2 are narrow money. M3 and M4 are broad money. NCERT calls M3 the most commonly used measure.  
**SCREEN:** NCERT: M1 = cash + demand deposits · M3 = M1 + time deposits · M2, M4 add post-office deposits · M3 most commonly used  
**CLAIMS:** ECO16-N25 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-R08 (CONFIRMED), ECO16-N23 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** NCERT's definitions. RBI's own table also adds a small 'other deposits with RBI' (R08); not narrated.

**VOICE:** Now we can answer the opening question. On the thirty-first of July 2026, M3, a broad measure of money, split as follows. About thirteen percent was cash with the public.
