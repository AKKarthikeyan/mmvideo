## Chapter 2

### b02 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1 · new term: magma
**PICTURE:** A cut-away mountain in atlas relief: a glowing chamber deep below labelled MAGMA, a red flow on the slope labelled LAVA; the ground line between them glows.  
**VOICE:** Start with the word. NCERT calls a volcano a place where gases, ash or melted rock escape to the ground. The United States Geological Survey, USGS, adds that the word also means the vent itself. It usually means the cone the vent builds around itself, too. Under the ground, melted rock is called magma. Once it erupts, it is called lava. Our Class 11 book starts calling it lava a little earlier, once it begins to rise. For the exam, keep the clean line: magma below, lava above.  
**SCREEN:** volcano = vent (+ usually its cone) · magma: underground · lava: erupted  
**CLAIMS:** V001 (CONFIRMED), V003 (CORRECTED)

### b03 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2 · new term: pyroclastic debris
**PICTURE:** An eruption sorts itself into labelled layers: heavy bombs falling close, ash drifting far, a pale plume of steam. A small exam card: the four items, all ticked (our answer).  
**VOICE:** So what comes out? NCERT lists lava flows, broken rock called pyroclastic debris, volcanic bombs, ash and dust, and gases. The gases it names are nitrogen and sulphur compounds, with a little chlorine, hydrogen and argon. That is the list the 2024 question tested. By NCERT's list, all four things it asked about come out. We have not read UPSC's own answer key, so that answer is ours. USGS says the biggest lumps, the bombs, usually land near the vent. The finest ash travels farthest. And an older USGS fact sheet adds a gas the textbook leaves out. More than nine-tenths of volcanic gas is water vapour.  
**SCREEN:** NCERT: lava · pyroclastic debris · bombs · ash & dust · gases | older USGS fact sheet: >90% of the gas is water vapour  
**CLAIMS:** V004 (CONFIRMED), V006 (CONFIRMED), V007 (CONFIRMED), V005 (CONFLICT)

## Chapter 3

### b05 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2 · new term: viscous
**PICTURE:** The girl shakes a bottle of fizzy drink with her thumb on top; lifts it; foam bursts out. Beside it, two jugs: water poured runs and bubbles pop; thick porridge holds big bubbles that burst. [chip: ANALOGY; limit: in magma the gas comes out as pressure falls, not by shaking]  
**VOICE:** Here is the switch, in the words of USGS. Thin, runny lava lets its gas escape quietly. Thick, pasty lava traps the gas, until it bursts out. Geologists call that thick lava viscous. USGS compares it to a fizzy drink. Shake the bottle with your thumb on top, then lift your thumb. The trapped gas rushes out, and the drink comes with it. The picture stops at one point. In magma, the gas is not shaken loose. It comes out of the melt as the pressure on it falls, as the magma rises.  
**SCREEN:** runny lava: gas escapes quietly · viscous lava: gas trapped → explosion (USGS)  
**CLAIMS:** V009 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** The limit sentence is ours (V009 note) and is shown as the analogy's limit.

### b06 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2 · new term: shield volcano
**PICTURE:** A wide, low dome in atlas relief, thin lava sheets layered like pages; a figure of the girl at its foot for scale. Then a thin jet of steam where sea water meets the vent.  
**VOICE:** So the lava decides the shape. Runny basalt lava spreads far before it cools. It builds a shield volcano: broad, with gentle slopes. Mauna Loa is the largest of them. It rises more than four thousand metres above the sea. NCERT notes one exception. A shield volcano can turn explosive if water gets into its vent.  
**SCREEN:** shield volcano: runny basalt · broad, gentle · Mauna Loa: 13,677 ft (~4,170 m) above sea level (USGS)  
**CLAIMS:** V012 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** 4,169 m is our conversion of USGS's 13,677 ft.

### b07 · *BUT* · rung 2 · new term: composite volcano
**PICTURE:** A steep cone cut open to show stripes of lava and ash; then a grey avalanche of hot cloud racing down its flank.  
**VOICE:** But sticky lava behaves differently. It erupts cooler, with ash, and builds up in layers. That builds a steep cone called a composite volcano, or a stratovolcano. Mount Fuji in Japan is one. So are Mount St Helens in the United States, and Pinatubo. These are the ones that often explode. When they do, a pyroclastic flow can race down the side: a fast avalanche of hot ash, rock and gas. USGS says it can be hotter than eight hundred degrees Celsius.  
**SCREEN:** composite volcano (stratovolcano): steep, lava + ash layers · Fuji, St Helens, Pinatubo | pyroclastic flow up to 1,500 °F (~815 °C)  
**CLAIMS:** V013 (CONFIRMED), V008 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** 815 °C is our conversion of USGS's 1,500 °F.

### b08 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: caldera
**PICTURE:** Five cards on the girl's desk, each a small relief drawing: dome, steep cone, collapsed hollow with a blue lake, stacked lava sheets, a glowing crack on the sea floor.  
**VOICE:** NCERT names five kinds of volcano in all, by how they erupt and what they build. Shield and composite are two. The third is a caldera. When a volcano empties its magma chamber, the top can fall in, leaving a huge hollow. Crater Lake, in Oregon, sits in one that formed about six thousand eight hundred years ago. The fourth is a flood basalt province. Very runny lava pours out in many flows, for hundreds of kilometres. The fifth is the mid-ocean ridge volcano. Here lava erupts under the sea. USGS says scientists estimate that about three-quarters of all lava erupted on Earth comes out under the oceans.  
**SCREEN:** NCERT's 5: shield · composite · caldera · flood basalt province · mid-ocean ridge | ~¾ of erupted lava: under the sea (USGS estimate)  
**CLAIMS:** V011 (CONFIRMED), V014 (CONFIRMED), V016 (CONFIRMED), V018 (CONFIRMED)

## Chapter 4

### b10 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: intrusive forms
**PICTURE:** A cut-away of the crust: a huge granite body far below; above it, layers wear away over a fast clock until its dome shows as grey hills.  
**VOICE:** When lava cools on the surface, it makes volcanic rock. When magma cools underground, it makes plutonic rock. Underground it cools slowly, so its crystals grow bigger and the rock is coarser. NCERT calls the shapes it leaves underground intrusive forms. A batholith is a large granite body that cooled deep in the crust, part of an old magma chamber. We only see one after erosion strips away the rock above it.  
**SCREEN:** volcanic rock (surface) v plutonic rock (underground, coarser) · intrusive forms · batholith  
**CLAIMS:** V019 (CONFIRMED), V020 (CONFIRMED)

### b11 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: dyke
**PICTURE:** One labelled cut-away of the crust with all six shapes drawn in, each lighting up as it is named; the dyke glows as a vertical wall feeding lava sheets above.  
**VOICE:** A laccolith is a dome with a flat base, fed by a pipe from below. A lapolith is shaped like a saucer. A phacolith is a wavy body that sits in folded rock. A sill is a near-horizontal body; a thinner one is called a sheet. And a dyke is a wall, where magma filled a crack and hardened almost upright. NCERT says dykes are the commonest intrusive form in western Maharashtra. It says they are considered the feeders of the Deccan Traps.  
**SCREEN:** laccolith · lapolith · phacolith · sill · dyke | NCERT: dykes, commonest in western Maharashtra, considered the Deccan Traps' feeders  
**CLAIMS:** V021 (CONFIRMED), V023 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** NCERT is the only source read for these shapes (V020, V021 notes): marked for the reviewer (R1).


## Chapter 5

### b12 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: Ring of Fire
**PICTURE:** A world map with the plates outlined; red dots gather along the edges and crowd round the Pacific in a ring. A slice view: an ocean plate sliding under a continent, a volcano above.  
**VOICE:** Now, where do volcanoes sit? Most active volcanoes stand at or near the edges of the plates that make up Earth's shell. Where an ocean plate meets a continental plate, the ocean plate sinks beneath it. That gives volcanoes and earthquakes. Where plates move apart, magma rises and makes new crust, as along the mid-ocean ridges. The busiest belt rings the Pacific Ocean. USGS says more than half the world's active volcanoes circle it. NCERT calls it the rim of fire. It is better known as the Ring of Fire.  
**SCREEN:** most active volcanoes: near plate boundaries | Pacific Ring of Fire: more than ½ of active volcanoes (USGS)  
**CLAIMS:** V024 (CONFIRMED), V025 (CONFIRMED), V026 (CONFIRMED)

### b13 · *THEREFORE* · rung 4
**PICTURE:** The slice view again: a sticky, steep cone over the sinking plate; then across the ocean, a ridge splitting with runny lava welling up.  
**VOICE:** Now the switch and the map join up. USGS says most active volcanoes stand near boundaries where one plate sinks under another. The volcanoes over sinking plates, like St Helens and Pinatubo, are composite cones. The ridges, where plates pull apart, pour out runny basalt.  
**SCREEN:** where a plate sinks: composite cones (St Helens, Pinatubo) · where plates part: runny basalt (ridges)  
**CLAIMS:** V025 (CONFIRMED), V013 (CONFIRMED), V018 (CONFIRMED)

### b14 · *BUT* · rung 4 · new term: hotspot
**PICTURE:** The question mark from the start lands on Hawaii. A cut-away: a plume of heat under a moving plate, islands forming one by one and sliding away like a conveyor; the oldest ones worn low.  
**VOICE:** But remember Hawaii. Its islands formed more than three thousand two hundred kilometres from the nearest plate boundary. So where does its magma come from? In 1963, the geologist J. Tuzo Wilson suggested an answer: a hotspot. A fixed hot region deep in the mantle melts rock beneath the Pacific plate. As the plate drifts over it, the hotspot builds a new island, and the older ones are carried away. Kauai, far along the chain, has oldest rocks about five and a half million years old. A hotspot track has also been linked to India's Deccan Traps. We follow that story in part two.  
**SCREEN:** Hawaii: 3,200+ km from the nearest plate boundary · hotspot (J. Tuzo Wilson, 1963) · Kauai's oldest rocks ~5.5 million years  
**CLAIMS:** V027 (CONFIRMED), V028 (CONFLICT)

It pours from Hawaii's hotspot, and from the ocean ridges. Sticky lava traps its gas until it bursts. Over sinking plates stand composite cones like St Helens and Pinatubo. For the exam, write it this way. Magma is melted rock underground. Lava is magma that has erupted. Thin lava erupts gently. Thick, viscous lava traps gas and explodes. Magma that cools underground leaves intrusive forms: batholith, laccolith, lapolith, phacolith, sill and dyke. Most active volcanoes sit near plate boundaries. More than half of them ring the Pacific. Hotspots, like Hawaii's, can build volcanoes far from any boundary.
