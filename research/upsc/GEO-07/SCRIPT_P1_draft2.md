## A. The script

### 01 · Cold open (0:00-0:35) · feeling: curiosity
**PICTURE:** First frame, one second, no label: the Earth from space with a thin white band of cloud near the equator. Cut to a cartoon street in June. Heat shimmer. The kid sits on a doorstep with a notebook, writing fast. The first fat drops hit the dust and everyone looks up.
**KID (speech bubble, then voiced if AK adds a second voice):** "Easy. Land hot, sea cool, wind comes. Done."
**VOICE:** Every year, India waits for one thing. Rain. And most of it comes in just four months. Three of every four raindrops India gets in a year fall between June and September.
Ask anyone why. You'll hear the same answer. The one our friend just wrote. "The land gets hot. The sea stays cool. So the wind blows in from the sea, carrying rain." It's in our textbooks. It sounds right.
But here is a strange fact. India is hotter in May than in July. And the hottest part of India gets hardly any monsoon rain at all.
So if heat is the engine, why does the rain wait? Today we find out what really pulls the monsoon in.
**SCREEN:** 3 of 4 raindrops · June to September · May > July?
**CLAIMS:** M046 (74.9%, "three of every four" is our rounding), M003, M008, M102
**RUNG:** opens the question · **TERM:** none

### 02 · The word (0:35-1:05) · wonder
**PICTURE:** An old sailing ship on the Arabian Sea. A wind arrow points one way, then swings right round.
**VOICE:** First, the word. "Monsoon" comes from the Arabic word *mausim*. It means season. In this part of the world, the main winds change direction with the seasons. In one season they blow one way. In another, they turn around.
That's what a monsoon is. Not just rain. A seasonal reversal of the winds.
**SCREEN:** mausim = season · winds reverse
**CLAIMS:** M002, M001
**RUNG:** 1-2 · **TERM:** monsoon

### 03 · Weather or climate (1:05-1:25) · calm
**PICTURE:** A single rainy day on a calendar, then a whole year of calendars stacking up.
**VOICE:** One more word before we begin. A single wet afternoon is weather: the state of the air right now. Climate is the average of the weather over many years. The monsoon is climate. It is the rhythm of India's year.
**SCREEN:** weather = today · climate = the pattern
**CLAIMS:** M101
**RUNG:** 2 · **TERM:** climate

### 04 · Why land heats faster (1:25-2:25) · curiosity
**PICTURE:** The kid on a beach at noon, hopping on burning sand ("Ow, ow, ow!"), then stepping into cool water with a sigh.
**VOICE:** Now the textbook story. Have you walked on a beach at noon? The sand burns your feet. The water is cool.
Land heats up fast and cools down fast. Water heats up slowly and cools down slowly.
At the beach this makes a daily wind. By day, warm air rises over the hot sand. Cooler air from the sea slides in to take its place. That's a sea breeze. At night it turns around, because now the land is cooler than the sea. That's a land breeze.
**SCREEN:** land: fast · water: slow · sea breeze → by day
**CLAIMS:** M006, M005
**RUNG:** 1-3 · **TERM:** sea breeze

### 05 · Pressure: why air moves (2:25-3:15) · curiosity
**PICTURE:** A crowded room and an empty room with a door between. People drift into the empty one.
**VOICE:** Why does the air move at all? Air presses down on everything. Scientists call that push air pressure.
When air warms and rises, it leaves less air pushing down below. That is a low-pressure area. Where cool air sinks, it piles up. That is a high-pressure area.
Air flows from high pressure to low pressure, like people leaving a crowded room for an empty one. **[analogy: it shows the direction of flow; air is not "choosing", it is pushed]** The bigger the difference, the stronger the wind. Scientists call that difference the pressure gradient.
**SCREEN:** HIGH → LOW · pressure gradient
**CLAIMS:** M096
**RUNG:** 1-3 · **TERM:** pressure gradient

### 06 · The giant sea breeze idea (3:15-4:15) · confidence
**PICTURE:** The beach zooms out. The beach becomes India. The kid becomes a tiny dot. One giant arrow sweeps from the Indian Ocean onto the land.
**VOICE:** Now make the beach as big as a country. In summer, the whole of India heats up. The Indian Ocean stays cooler. So a huge low forms over the land, and a high over the sea. Moist wind blows from sea to land, and it rains.
That is the classic explanation. The scientist Halley suggested it in 1686, and it made the monsoon a gigantic land-sea breeze. Our Class 9 textbook says the south-west monsoon is mainly caused by this unequal heating of land and sea. Several scientists still treat it as the basic mechanism.
It is neat. It is simple. And it's where every student should start.
**SCREEN:** Halley, 1686 · "unequal heating"
**CLAIMS:** M007, M003, M004
**RUNG:** 3 · **TERM:** none new

### 07 · Why the wind arrives from the south-west (4:15-5:15) · wonder
**PICTURE:** A playground roundabout. A kid rolls a ball straight across. From above, its path curves.
**VOICE:** But look at a map of the monsoon winds and something odd appears. The wind doesn't blow straight north into India. It comes from the south-west. Why?
Because the Earth spins. Roll a ball across a spinning roundabout and, to someone riding it, the ball seems to curve. **[analogy: on Earth the curve comes from the planet's rotation, and it is zero at the equator and strongest at the poles]** Scientists call this the Coriolis force. North of the equator it bends moving air to the right. South of it, to the left.
The winds that feed our monsoon start south of the equator, as the south-east trade winds. They cross the equator between about 40 and 60 degrees east, near the coast of Africa. There, the Coriolis force bends them to the right. And the south-east wind becomes the south-west monsoon.
**SCREEN:** Coriolis: right in the north · SE trades → SW monsoon
**CLAIMS:** M096, M026
**RUNG:** 2-5 · **TERM:** Coriolis force
**EXAM LINE (on screen, end of scene):** SE trades cross the equator and turn into the SW monsoon.

### 08 · The first crack (5:15-6:30) · worry
**PICTURE:** May. The kid on a baking rooftop in north-west India beside an empty bucket, thermometer climbing. Speech bubble: "If heat brings rain, this bucket should be full by now." July: the thermometer drops a little, the bucket fills.
**VOICE:** So far, so good. Now let's test the idea. If heat drives the monsoon, then the hotter the land, the stronger the pull. Right? Think about it for a second.
In 1921, the meteorologist Simpson pointed out three problems with this idea. India is hotter in May than in July, yet the heavy rain comes later. The hottest part of India, the north-west, gets hardly any monsoon rain. And in years when the rain failed, the land was on average hotter, not cooler.
Nearly a century later, the Indian scientist Sulochana Gadgil checked the modern record. Between 1950 and 2014, years with hotter Indian summers tended to have weaker monsoons, not stronger ones.
If heat were the engine, more heat should mean more rain. It doesn't.
**SCREEN:** May hotter than July · NW: hottest, driest · hotter years, weaker rain
**CLAIMS:** M008 (Simpson 1921, as reported by Gadgil 2003), M009, M102
**RUNG:** 4 · **TERM:** none new
**HEDGE KEPT:** "tended to", one tendency, not a rule.

### 09 · NCERT's own warning (6:30-7:05) · curiosity
**PICTURE:** The kid flips a textbook page. A small note glows.
**VOICE:** Here's the twist. Our Class 11 textbook already warns us. After describing the heating, it says no single theory has fully explained the monsoon. And it says the breakthrough came when scientists stopped looking at India alone, and studied the whole planet.
So let's zoom out.
**SCREEN:** "no single theory" · look at the whole planet
**CLAIMS:** M004
**RUNG:** 4 · **TERM:** none

### 10 · The planet's rain belt (7:05-8:35) · wonder
**PICTURE:** The same view as the first frame of the video: the globe from space, and the white band of clouds near the equator, now glowing. Arrows rise in the band, flow out high up, sink near 30 degrees, and return low down.
**VOICE:** Remember the very first picture in this video? That white band. From space, the Earth wears a belt of cloud near the equator. Here the sun heats hardest. Warm, wet air rises, up to about 14 kilometres. Up high it flows away towards the poles. Near 30 degrees north and south it sinks again, and piles up into high pressure. Then it flows back along the surface towards the equator. These returning winds are the trade winds.
Think of a pot of water on a stove. Water rises over the flame, spreads out, sinks at the sides, and flows back to the middle. **[analogy: a real loop of rising and sinking, but the Earth's turns as the planet spins, which is why the trade winds slant]** This giant loop is called the Hadley cell.
Where the trade winds from both sides meet, air is forced up. And rising air is what makes cloud and rain. That meeting line is called the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, or ITCZ. It is the planet's rain belt.
**SCREEN:** rising air · sinking at 30° · Hadley cell · ITCZ = rain belt
**CLAIMS:** M097, M013, M103
**RUNG:** 1-3 · **TERM:** ITCZ (and Hadley cell, named with the pot)

### 11 · The belt follows the sun (8:35-9:30) · wonder
**PICTURE:** The year runs fast. The sun's overhead spot slides north and south. The cloud belt slides after it like a shadow.
**VOICE:** Here is the key. The rain belt does not stay put. The pressure and wind belts move with the sun. In our summer they shift north. In our winter they slide back south.
Our Class 11 book says the most striking effect of this seasonal shift is seen in the monsoons. And over India, the shift is huge. In July, the rain belt sits over the Gangetic plain, at about 20 to 25 degrees north. There it has a special name: the monsoon trough.
So: the sun moves north, the belt moves north, and the rain moves with it.
**SCREEN:** belts follow the sun · July: 20–25° N · monsoon trough
**CLAIMS:** M095, M098, M013
**RUNG:** 3 · **TERM:** monsoon trough

### 12 · The click (9:30-10:40) · PEAK · awe
**PICTURE:** Split screen. Left: the giant sea-breeze arrow, fading. Right: the glowing belt sliding up over India, pulling the trade winds across the equator behind it.
**VOICE:** So here's the new picture. The monsoon is not India sucking in the sea breeze. It's the Earth's rain belt, moving north with the sun and settling over our subcontinent for the summer. The winds that cross the equator are being pulled into that moving belt.
Scientists who reviewed the research in 2020 put it this way. Monsoons used to be seen as giant sea breezes. Recent theory and observations support seeing them as seasonal shifts of the tropical rain belt. They call it a shift in understanding. Gadgil goes further, and argues that the sea-breeze view should be abandoned. Not everyone agrees, and the heating story is still in many textbooks.
Now look back at the strange facts. In July the rain belt is overhead, and rising air fills the sky with cloud and rain. Our textbook says the burst of the monsoon can cut day temperatures by 5 to 8 degrees. The north-west sits at the far edge of the belt, where there is hardly any rain, and that is exactly where Gadgil finds the hottest land. The rain follows the belt, not the heat.
**SCREEN:** monsoon = the rain belt moving north · "a shift in understanding"
**CLAIMS:** M011, M010 (CONFLICT: Gadgil's view, named as Gadgil's), M007, M043, M102
**RUNG:** 3-4 · **TERM:** none new
**NOTE:** the earlier draft's "dry, sinking air" line was cut: no source states it. Gadgil 2018 p.7 supports the edge-of-the-belt wording.

### 13 · Heat still has a job (10:40-11:40) · balance
**PICTURE:** North-west India and Pakistan under a shimmering dome. A thin low-pressure swirl sits near the ground with clear sky above. A line runs from it to the Bay of Bengal.
**VOICE:** Does that mean heat doesn't matter? No. Heat still shapes the system. Let's meet the machine's parts, one at a time.
Over the deserts of north-west India and Pakistan, summer heat makes a shallow low-pressure area called the heat low. It's only about one and a half kilometres deep. It brings plenty of cloud but little rain.
From that heat low, the monsoon trough stretches all the way to the head of the Bay of Bengal. When the trough sits south of its usual place, most of India gets heavy rain. When it moves north to the Himalayan foothills, the rest of India gets a break. More on breaks in part two.
And some scientists, like Wu and colleagues in 2012, find that land-sea heating still sets the monsoon south of about 20 degrees north. So heating is not wrong. It's part of a bigger machine.
**SCREEN:** heat low · trough: south = rain · north = break
**CLAIMS:** M015, M016, M017, M099 (CONFLICT, named as Wu et al.'s finding)
**RUNG:** 4 · **TERM:** heat low

### 14 · The machine's other parts (11:40-12:40) · curiosity
**PICTURE:** A cartoon map of the Indian Ocean. A high-pressure "H" near Madagascar pushes. A fast low ribbon of wind runs up the Somali coast and bends east to India.
**VOICE:** Two more parts make the machine work. Far south, near 30 degrees south and 50 degrees east, sits a high-pressure area called the Mascarene High. When it gets stronger, it pushes harder.
That push feeds a fast river of low-level wind, about one to one and a half kilometres up. It runs from near Madagascar to the coast of Somalia, then swings across the Arabian Sea. It's called the Somali jet. It reaches India's west coast in June and is strongest in July. When it strengthens, the monsoon strengthens over peninsular India.
**SCREEN:** Mascarene High · Somali jet · June → July
**CLAIMS:** M020, M018, M019
**RUNG:** 2-3 · **TERM:** Somali jet (Mascarene High named with it)

### 15 · The wall in the north (12:40-13:35) · tension
**PICTURE:** The Himalaya rises like a giant wall. Cold wind from Central Asia bumps into it. Wet clouds on the Indian side pile up against it.
**VOICE:** Then there's the Himalaya. Our textbook calls it a climatic divide. It shields India from cold winds from the north. And it traps the monsoon winds, so they drop their rain inside the subcontinent.
Behind the mountains lies the high Tibetan Plateau. How it matters is still argued. Some scientists say the plateau works like a stove, heating the air above it. Others, like Boos and Kuang, find in their models that the mountains matter mainly as a wall, keeping cold, dry air out. Wu and colleagues argue the plateau's heating drives the eastern part of the South Asian monsoon. The question is open. You should know both sides.
**SCREEN:** Himalaya: shield + trap · Tibet: stove or wall?
**CLAIMS:** M025, M024, M100, M099
**RUNG:** 4 · **TERM:** none new

### 16 · The jets and the burst (13:35-14:25) · tension
**PICTURE:** High above India, a thin fast stream of wind, labelled "east to west", near the latitude of Chennai. Below, the sky splits open in thunder.
**VOICE:** Finally, the jet streams. Very high above India in July, near the top of the weather layer, blows a fast wind from east to west. It's called the Tropical Easterly Jet. It runs from Vietnam to west Africa, near the latitude of Chennai.
When the monsoon arrives, it often comes suddenly, with thunder and lightning. This is called the burst. Our textbook says the jet streams changing at this time are held responsible for the burst. But in the research we checked, no source shows the jets cause it. They change together with the onset. Think of them as part of the same big shift, not the trigger.
**SCREEN:** Tropical Easterly Jet · the burst
**CLAIMS:** M021, M043, M022 (CONFLICT, NCERT's statement named as NCERT's)
**RUNG:** 4 · **TERM:** Tropical Easterly Jet
**NOTE:** the height in km was cut: the ledger gives 100-150 hPa, not kilometres. "Near the top of the weather layer" is our wording and needs the S5 check.

### 17 · The exam answer (14:25-15:05) · clarity
**PICTURE:** The kid's notebook from the cold open. The kid crosses out "Easy." and the clean answer fills the page line by line.
**VOICE:** So if an exam asks, "What causes the Indian monsoon?", here is a full answer.
The south-east trade winds cross the equator and turn into the south-west monsoon. The classic explanation is unequal heating of land and sea. Modern research sees the monsoon as the seasonal northward shift of the ITCZ, the monsoon trough, over the subcontinent. The heat low, the Mascarene High, the Somali jet and the Himalaya shape its strength. The role of the jets and of the Tibetan Plateau is still debated.
**SCREEN:** five lines, each two to five words, appearing with the voice
**CLAIMS:** M026, M003, M011, M013, M015, M020, M018, M025, M022, M024
**RUNG:** 5

**PICTURE:** Back to the doorstep from the cold open. Rain falls. The kid closes the notebook, looks up past the clouds, and imagines the glowing belt overhead.
**VOICE:** So the next time the rain comes, remember. It isn't the sea rushing in because the land got hot. It's the whole planet's rain belt, following the sun, coming to visit.
In part two, we follow it across India. Where it lands first, why Rajasthan waits, and what "normal" rain really means.
