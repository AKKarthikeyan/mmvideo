## Item 1. Jets and the monsoon burst

- Gemini said: (no claim given; lead asked for one open review).
- Verdict: FOUND (partial). No review I can open states that the jet shift is the cause; the best open review treats it as a companion of onset.
- Source: Ding, Y. 2007, "The Variability of the Asian Summer Monsoon", J. Meteorol. Soc. Japan 85B:21-54.
  https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jmsj/85B/0/85B_0_21/_pdf  File: `jmsj2007_asian_monsoon_variability.pdf`, `.txt`.
- Where: PDF p.7 (journal p.27), the paragraph on the Indian summer monsoon (ISM) onset.
- What it says: during the ISM onset the South Asian high at 200 hPa develops and moves north-west. That sets up the tropical
  easterly jet on its south side and a rapid northward jump of the upper westerly jet on its north side, over the northern Tibetan Plateau.
  The review calls the question of the deep heat source in the north "a central issue of the onset" and says the cited studies find
  that heating over the Tibetan Plateau plays an important role in triggering onset (Ding lists Yanai et al. 1992 among them).
  Ding does not argue the jets cause the burst; they are listed as parts of the circulation change that arrives with onset.
- Also opened: Yanai, Li and Song 1992, JMSJ 70(1B) (`yanai1992_jmsj.pdf`,
  https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jmsj1965/70/1B/70_1B_319/_pdf). Abstract says the onset is an interaction process of
  Plateau heating and the larger monsoon system. It describes the westerly jet weakening and the easterly jet forming to its south (PDF p.8).
- Caution: Ding 2007 and Boos and Kuang 2010 (already in sources) disagree on Plateau heating versus insulation. See item 16.
  NCERT's "jet withdraws, so monsoon bursts" is a description of what happens together, not a settled cause in what I could open.
  Not opened: Wu et al. 2007, J. Hydrometeor. (AMS PDF, request refused).

## Item 6. Indian Ocean Dipole

- Gemini said: (leads: Saji et al. 1999 abstract; Ashok et al. 2001).
- Verdicts: Saji 1999 abstract MATCHES the idea (found). Ashok 2001 CANNOT OPEN (Wiley refused). Positive-IOD offset: FOUND in three open sources.
- Saji et al. 1999, Nature 401:360-363, doi 10.1038/43854, published 23 Sept 1999 (`saji1999.txt`, https://www.nature.com/articles/43854; abstract only, full text paywalled).
  Abstract: 40 years of data show a dipole mode in the Indian Ocean (cool sea surface off Sumatra, warm in the west), shown to be independent of
  El Nino/Southern Oscillation, accounting for about 12% of Indian Ocean sea-surface-temperature variability, with severe rain in East Africa and drought in Indonesia in active years.
  It does not mention the Indian monsoon.
- Offset, source 1 (publisher of the forecast): IMD FAQ on ENSO and IOD (ledger `sources/imd_faq_enso_iod.txt`, Q28, PDF p.15). "Yes": a positive IOD can partially,
  and sometimes substantially, offset the El Nino drying effect; 1997 was a strong El Nino year with normal monsoon rain.
  A negative IOD with El Nino raises the chance of below-normal rain.
- Offset, source 2 (open paper): Ummenhofer et al. 2011, Environ. Res. Lett. 6:034006. https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/6/3/034006/pdf
  File `iod_el_nino_monsoon_iop2011.pdf`. PDF p.3 (journal p.2) reviews that model experiments found a positive IOD coincident with El Nino counteracted the expected
  monsoon reduction. PDF p.5: in 1877-2006 data, El Nino years with a positive IOD generally had normal monsoon rainfall; "pure" El Nino years mostly had large deficits.
  Their reference [8] is Ashok, Guan and Yamagata 2001 (GRL 28:4499-4502) and [9] is Ashok et al. 2004 (J. Climate 17:3141-55, closed; request refused). These are what Gemini pointed to.
- Not opened: Ashok et al. 2001 itself (Wiley 403).

## Item 7. IPCC AR6 WG1 Chapter 8

- File: `ipcc_ch08.pdf` (final published chapter, 156 pages, PDF created 9 June 2022; https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter08.pdf).

  aerosol forcing has dominated the recent decrease in summer monsoon rain, against the expected GHG-driven rise (high confidence).
  Body text, same page: model studies say the decline in the second half of the 20th century is dominated by Northern Hemisphere aerosol effects, with smaller volcanic contributions.
- (b) Projected increase. Verdict: CORRECTED (two confidence levels). Executive Summary, PDF p.4 (printed p.1058): "Summer monsoon precipitation is projected to increase for the South,
  South East and East Asian monsoon domains" with MEDIUM confidence (the same sentence block gives medium confidence that North American monsoon rain falls).
  But Section 8.4.2.4.1 summary, PDF p.75 (printed p.1129), says HIGH confidence that SAsiaM rain increases in the 21st century under the CMIP6 higher-emissions scenarios,
  mostly mid and long term. Section 8.4.2.4.1, PDF p.73 (printed p.1127): all CMIP6 scenarios show an increase except SSP1-1.9, where the ensemble mean change is negative.
  Use the section for South Asia only ("high" with the scenario qualifier) and the Executive Summary for the grouped Asian sentence ("medium"). Do not mix them.
- (c) Executive Summary, PDF p.3 (printed p.1057). Verdict: MATCHES, with HIGH confidence. Wording: observed changes in regional monsoon rain, especially over South Asia, East Asia and West Africa,
  have been limited over much of the 20th century, because GHG-driven increases were counteracted by aerosol-driven decreases (high confidence). Cross-references {8.3.1.3, 8.3.2.4, Box 8.1}.
- Note for the script: (c) says the change was limited over much of the 20th century; (a) says a decline is evident in the second half. They are consistent only if the offset is described as breaking down late in the century; the chapter does not spell that out in these sentences.

## Item 9. Roxy et al. 2017

- Gemini said: 75% rise in extreme events, 150 mm/day, central India, 1950-2015, 10% fall in mean rain.
- Verdict: MATCHES on all five, with a distinction Gemini blurred.
- Source: Roxy, M. K. et al. 2017, Nature Communications 8:708, published 3 Oct 2017 (open access).
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-00744-9  File `roxy2017.html`, `roxy2017.txt` (no PDF page numbers; section "Results", first subsection).
- 75%: frequency of extreme rain events (daily rain of 150 mm or more) over central India "increased by about 75%" over 1950-2015, significant at 95% confidence, about 13 events a decade.
  Grid points are 0.25 degree cells from the IMD daily gridded product (6,955 stations); the box in Fig. 1e/1f is 75-85E, 19-26N.
- The "threefold" in the title is a different quantity: widespread extreme events (extreme rain on at least ten grid cells the same day), described as a trend "consistent with a threefold increase" for 1950-2012 in the text and 1950-2015 in the abstract.
  So 75% (single-cell extreme events) and threefold (widespread events) should not be used for the same thing.
- 10%: summer mean rainfall over the central Indian region fell about 10% in 1950-2015, with 20-30% falls at some grid points. The introduction says a 10-20% decline over central India.
- Season: June-September ("northern summer").

## Items 10, 17, 18, 19. Drought year, "normal", NE monsoon, seasons

Sources: IMD Pune glossary (https://www.imdpune.gov.in/Reports/glossary.pdf, `imd_glossary.pdf`, 6 pages, undated) and Forecaster's Guide
(https://www.imdpune.gov.in/Reports/forecaster_guide.pdf, `imd_forecaster_guide.pdf`).

- Item 10, all-India drought year. Verdict: FOUND.
  Forecaster's Guide, Technical Circular No. 2/2007 (printed p.84; DDGM (WF), May 2007): a year with all-India rainfall deficiency of more than 10% and 20-40% of the country under drought is an All India Drought Year;
  spatial coverage above 40% makes it a Severe Drought Year. It supersedes earlier instructions. Glossary p.2 repeats it.
  Drought year here needs both conditions (deficiency above 10% and spatial coverage), and "drought conditions" per area are not defined in these two pages.
- Item 17, what "normal" means. Verdict: both are right, because they are different scales.
  - Scale A (realised all-India seasonal rain): IMD FAQ Q4 (ledger `imd_monsoonfaq.txt`, PDF p.1): normal when the all-India June-September rainfall is within plus or minus 10% of the LPA, i.e. 90-110%;
    below normal under 90%, above normal over 110%. Glossary p.2 says the same. Basis: the standard deviation is about 9 cm on an LPA of 88 cm (the FAQ's LPA is the older 1961-2010 base, 880.6 mm; the 2026 forecast uses 87 cm for 1971-2020 and the 2025 report 868.6 mm).
  - Scale B (realised rain for a sub-division, district, week, month or day): FAQ Q3 and Forecaster's Guide printed p.50 (circular of 9 Feb 1990): excess +20% or more; normal -19% to +19%; deficient -20% to -59%; scanty (FAQ: large deficient) -60% or less.
    FAQ adds large excess at +60% or more. IMD's 2025 report applies this to months ("normal (within +/-19% of LPA)", PDF p.9).
  - Scale C (the seasonal forecast, five probability categories): IMD Long Range Forecast press release, 13 April 2026 (ledger `imd_lrf2026_pressrelease_13apr.pdf`, PDF p.3).
    Deficient under 90; below normal 90-95; normal 96-104; above normal 105-110; excess over 110 (% of LPA, LPA 87 cm for 1971-2020). Forecast probabilities 35/31/27/6/1; climatological 16/17/33/16/17.
  So the forecast "normal" (96-104) is a narrower band than the realised "normal" (90-110). The word "below normal" also changes meaning: under 90 on Scale A, but 90-95 on Scale C.
  The 2025 Monsoon Report calls 108% of LPA "normal" (PDF p.6) and, on PDF p.69, "above normal" for the same 108% in the sub-division summary. The first fits Scale A; I could not tell from the report which scale the second sentence uses.
  IMD itself mixes the two in one report, which is likely the source of the confusion. The FAQ and the glossary give no sentence that ties Scale C to Scale A.
  Recommendation for scripts: always name the scale ("by IMD's all-India 90-110% rule" or "in the five-way forecast table"), and say 108% is "normal" on A and "above normal" on C.
- Item 18, NE monsoon season. Verdict: IMD is October-December.
  Glossary p.2 describes the Northeast Monsoon as "the period October to December" and gives Tamil Nadu coastal districts about 60% of their annual rain in this season.
  Forecaster's Guide printed p.8: "Post-monsoon (NE monsoon Season)" October, November, December. NE monsoon onset is not to be declared before 10 October (Appendix 5.3, circular of 7 June 1988, later modified by Circular 3/2006; the page prints no number in the text layer).
  The NCERT Grade 9 Part 1 text says "winter monsoon ... October to February" (ledger `ncert_class9/iest103.txt`, printed p.49). I found no IMD page in these two documents that says February.
- Item 19, season months. Verdict: IMD is January-February, March-May, June-September, October-December.
  Forecaster's Guide printed p.8 and glossary p.1 both give winter Jan-Feb, pre-monsoon (hot weather) Mar-May, south-west monsoon June-September, post-monsoon Oct-Dec.
  NCERT Grade 9 Part 1, printed p.47, says IMD recognises four seasons, but gives winter December to early April, summer April to June, monsoon June to September, post-monsoon October to December.
  The NCERT months for winter and summer do not match the IMD documents. Teach IMD's months for statistics and note NCERT's different winter/summer split if the script quotes NCERT.
- Also: the glossary still lists the old normal dates (Delhi 29 June, entire country 15 July). See items 2 and 11.

## Item 12. Meghaduta, Brihatsamhita, Krishiparashara

Mark: a poem or manual noting the season is not a measured onset date. None of these texts gives a calendar date of onset in the modern sense.

    the Yaksha, arms bare after a gold bracelet slipped, sees "on the first day of Ashadha (June-July)" a cloud resting on the hilltop. The gloss "(June-July)" is the translator's.

  - The cloud is at Ramagiri (verse 1, Yaksha in exile) on that day. I did not check the traditional identification with Ramtek near Nagpur, so "central India" is NCERT's wording and is unverified here.
  - Path north (Jacob's verse numbers): v.13 the Yaksha will describe the route; v.17 Mt Amrakuta; v.19 river Narmada (Reva); v.24-25 Dasarna country and Vidisha on the Vetravati;
    v.28 "going to the North" with a detour via Ujjain; v.35 Mahakala temple; v.48 river Charmanvati; v.49 Brahmavarta and Kurukshetra; v.51 the Ganga at Kanakhala; v.53 its source in the Himalaya.

  - NCERT says "5th century CE". I did not verify the date of Kalidasa; scholars give a range and the 5th century is common. Mark unverified.
  - Verse numbering differs between editions. Quote "verse 2" with the edition named.
- Brihatsamhita (Varahamihira, 6th c. CE; date not verified here). Verdict: SUPPORTED as rain-prediction by omens and astronomy; no onset dates.
  English translation by V. Subrahmanya Sastri, 1946 (`brihatsamhita_sastri1946.txt`, https://archive.org/details/VarahamihirasBrihatSamhitaByVSubrahmanyaSastri; 1946, copyright status not checked).

  ch.21 conception (garbha) of clouds: slokas 6-7 say the signs are read from the Moon in Purvashadha starting from the first day of Margasira, and the "foetus" is born 195 days later;
  ch.22 retention of rain water by clouds; ch.23 quantity of rainfall; ch.24 Rohini-yoga (Moon with Rohini in the dark half of Ashadha; slokas 4-5);
  ch.25 Swati-yoga; ch.26 Ashadha-yoga (full moon of Ashadha: winds and a seed-weighing rite; sloka 14 says rain on the 4th day of the dark half after the Ashadha full moon makes the season good);
  ch.27 wind circle (said to be an insertion); ch.28 immediate rain. NCERT's "Brihatsamhita on rainfall prediction" is supported. The text predicts the amount of the season's rain, not an onset date.
- Krishiparashara. Verdict: SUPPORTED as a rain-forecast manual; no onset date.
  Majumdar and Banerji (eds. and trans.), Krsi-Parasara, Bibliotheca Indica no. 285, Asiatic Society, Calcutta, 1960

  Verses 30-33: monthly rainfall from the wind direction in Pausa. Verse 43: Phalguna, Rohini. Further passages on Asadha (about verse 60) and Sravana. The introduction says the forecasts are of "annual rainfall" and "the practical value ... has got to be tested".
  The editors do not claim these are measured data.
- Where the NCERT line actually sits: Grade 9 Part 1 (not Part 2), ch.3 "Atmosphere and Climate", printed p.52 (ledger `ncert_class9/iest103.txt`, line 754, "DON'T MISS OUT" box). It names the Meghaduta only; I found no mention of Krishiparashara or Brihatsamhita in that file.

## Item 16. Tibetan Plateau: heating or barrier

- Gemini said: (asked for each paper's main conclusion).
- Verdict: both FOUND.
- Boos and Kuang 2013, Scientific Reports 3:1192, published 1 Feb 2013 (open). https://www.nature.com/articles/srep01192  File `boos_kuang2013.txt`.
  Main conclusion (abstract): in their climate model the monsoon strength is more sensitive to surface heat fluxes from non-elevated India than to fluxes from adjacent elevated terrain.
  They read this as support for orography acting as a thermal insulator that shields the warm region from cold dry air, not as a heat source. Monsoons respond most to heat sources under the thermal maximum.
  The Discussion also rebuts Wu et al. 2012's "air-pump" idea, as relying on a CISK-type feedback. They agree Boos and Kuang 2010 did not test sensible heat from Himalayan slopes.
- Wu et al. 2012, Scientific Reports 2:404, "Thermal Controls on the Asian Summer Monsoon", published 11 May 2012 (open). https://www.nature.com/articles/srep00404  File `wu2012.txt`.
  Main conclusion (abstract): from observations and model runs, the Asian summer monsoon is controlled mainly by thermal forcing, and large-scale mechanical forcing by orography is "not essential".
  The South Asian monsoon south of 20N is set by land-sea thermal contrast; its northern part by heating of the Iranian Plateau; the East Asian monsoon and the eastern part of the South Asian monsoon by heating of the Tibetan Plateau.
  The model SAMIL is a GCM from the Institute of Atmospheric Physics, Beijing. Experiments remove surface sensible heating above 500 m.
- Reading for the script: the two papers disagree on how mountains matter (Boos and Kuang: thermal insulation, with little role for elevated heating; Wu et al.: sensible heating of the plateaus matters in the north and east, not the barrier).
  Both agree that "mountains as a mechanical wall that blocks air" is not the main story. Do not present either as settled.
