## Chapter 2

### b02 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1 · new term: justified claim
**PICTURE:** The girl's notebook: two columns, WANTS and RIGHTS; a phone slides into WANTS, a well into RIGHTS.  
**VOICE:** So what is a right? NCERT's answer is short. A right is a justified claim, something others must accept as fair. Not every want is a right. Wanting a phone is not a right. Not being thrown out of a public well is. Rights are mostly claims on the state. They tell it what it must do, and what it must not do.  
**SCREEN:** NCERT: a right = a justified claim · rights tell the state what it must and must not do  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-K01 (CONFIRMED), POL03-K03 (CONFIRMED)

### b03 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1-2 · new term: bill of rights
**PICTURE:** A scroll unrolls into the Constitution's Part III; a vote counter tries to push it and stops at a glass wall; a courthouse door opens.  
**VOICE:** Some rights are written into the Constitution itself. NCERT calls such a list a bill of rights. NCERT says the Motilal Nehru committee asked for one as early as nineteen twenty-eight. Fundamental Rights are in Part Three. A majority cannot vote them away by an ordinary law. And if they are broken, you can go to court.  
**SCREEN:** NCERT: Motilal Nehru committee demanded a bill of rights, 1928 · Part III · courts enforce it  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-K06 (CONFIRMED), POL03-K08 (CONFIRMED), POL03-K09 (CONFIRMED)

### b04 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2 · new term: the State
**PICTURE:** Buildings light up one by one: Parliament, a State assembly, a municipal office; then a public-sector corporation joins them under one roof label THE STATE.  
**VOICE:** But who must respect these rights? Article twelve calls it the State. That means the governments and legislatures of India and of each State, and local and other authorities. In nineteen eighty, the Supreme Court read it wider. A body that works as an arm of the government counts too.  
**SCREEN:** Article 12: 'the State' · Ajay Hasia (1980): an instrumentality or agency of the Government counts  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-A01 (CONFIRMED), POL03-A02 (CONFIRMED)

### b05 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2-3
**PICTURE:** A rulebook with pages labelled ORDINANCE, RULE, NOTIFICATION, CUSTOM; the page that crosses a right fades to grey with VOID stamped on it.  
**VOICE:** And Article thirteen gives the rights their teeth. A law that takes away a Fundamental Right is void, to that extent. Laws made before the Constitution are tested too. And law here is wide. It includes an ordinance, a rule, a notification, even a custom with the force of law. UPSC Prelims has tested this. In twenty twenty-six, what counts as law here. In twenty twenty-five, whether an ordinance can take away a Fundamental Right.  
**SCREEN:** Article 13: a law that breaks Part III is void · 'law' includes ordinance, rule, notification, custom · Prelims 2025, 2026  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-A03 (CONFIRMED), POL03-A04 (CONFIRMED), POL03-A05 (CONFIRMED), POL03-A09 (CONFIRMED)

### b06 · *BUT* · rung 3
**PICTURE:** The Constitution as a building: a crane can replace bricks, but a glowing frame inside it cannot be touched. A card points to the basic structure episode.  
**VOICE:** But can Parliament change the rights themselves? Yes, by amending the Constitution. Article thirteen does not apply to an amendment. Still, in nineteen seventy-three, in Kesavananda Bharati, seven judges drew a line. An amendment cannot destroy the basic structure. That story has its own episode.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 13(4) (24th Amendment, 1971) · Kesavananda Bharati (1973), seven judges: no amendment may destroy the basic structure  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-A07 (CONFIRMED), POL03-A08 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 3

### b07 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1-2
**PICTURE:** Two circles: EVERY PERSON (a tourist, a worker, a child) and, inside it, CITIZENS; article numbers drop into each.  
**VOICE:** Now, who holds which right? Many people say every citizen has these rights. That is not quite how the text reads. Some rights belong to every person, citizen or not. Equality before law in Article fourteen is one. Life and personal liberty in Article twenty-one is another. Others are for citizens only, like the six freedoms of Article nineteen, and Articles fifteen and sixteen.  
**SCREEN:** Any person: Arts 14, 20, 21, 22, 25 · citizens only: Arts 15, 16, 19, 29  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-A10 (CONFIRMED), POL03-A11 (CONFIRMED), POL03-A12 (CORRECTED)

## Chapter 4

### b08 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2 · new term: equal protection
**PICTURE:** A judge's scale with a minister and a domestic worker on the same pan; then two queues of similar people treated alike.  
**VOICE:** Start the equality code with Article fourteen. The State shall not deny any person equality before the law, or the equal protection of the laws. NCERT explains the two phrases. Equality before law means the law treats everyone alike, whatever their rank. Equal protection means people in similar situations are treated alike. And in Maneka Gandhi, in nineteen seventy-eight, the lead opinion said equality is the opposite of arbitrary action.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 14: equality before the law · equal protection of the laws · Maneka Gandhi (1978), lead opinion: equality is opposed to arbitrariness  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-B01 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B03 (CONFIRMED)

### b09 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2-3
**PICTURE:** Back to the river steps from the start: the ghat now open to all; then a women's bus seat and a children's school ramp appear.  
**VOICE:** Article fifteen bars the State from discriminating against a citizen. Not on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Clause two goes further. No citizen can be shut out of shops, public restaurants, wells, tanks, bathing ghats and roads. That answers our first question, as we read it. The ghats are named because people had been kept from them. But clause three adds that the State can still make special provisions for women and children.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 15(1)-(2): no discrimination · named public places · Art. 15(3): special provisions for women and children  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-B04 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B05 (CONFIRMED), POL03-K13 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Pays off the opening plant (b00).

### b10 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** Three cards on a desk: a job notice open to all; a broken chain across a village well; a crossed-out title scroll beside a military medal and a degree.  
**VOICE:** Article sixteen gives every citizen equality of opportunity in public jobs. Article seventeen abolishes untouchability and forbids it in any form. Enforcing it is an offence punishable by law. The Protection of Civil Rights Act of nineteen fifty-five punishes it. A separate law of nineteen eighty-nine, the Prevention of Atrocities Act, lists atrocities against Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. And Article eighteen ends titles, except military or academic distinctions.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 16: equal opportunity in public jobs · Art. 17: untouchability abolished · PCR Act 1955 · PoA Act 1989 · Art. 18: titles ended  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-B08 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B11 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B14 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B13 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B12 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 5

### b11 · *BUT* · rung 3 · new term: adequately represented
**PICTURE:** The balance scale from the start steadies: the clause slides in as part of the EQUALITY pan, not against it.  
**VOICE:** But now the puzzle. Article sixteen, clause four, says nothing in it stops the State from reserving posts. The reservation is for any backward class which, in the State's opinion, is not adequately represented in its services. NCERT reads this plainly. It says reservation is not a violation of equality. And Article fifteen got a similar clause in nineteen fifty-one, by the First Amendment.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 16(4): a backward class not adequately represented, in the State's opinion · NCERT: not a violation of equality · Art. 15(4), 1951  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-B09 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B05 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** The turn: reservation is written inside the equality code.

### b12 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3-4 · new term: substantive equality
**PICTURE:** Two runners at different starting lines; a ramp brings one up to the line. A small balance labelled EFFICIENCY sits beside the track.  
**VOICE:** In plain words, as we read it, equality here means more than treating everyone the same. It means treating people in unequal situations differently, so they can start level. The Directive Principles point the same way. Article forty-six asks the State to promote, with special care, the interests of the weaker sections. And Article three thirty-five adds a balance. The claims of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes to services are weighed with the efficiency of administration.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 46: promote weaker sections 'with special care' · Art. 335: SC/ST claims, consistently with efficiency of administration  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-C18 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C17 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** 'Start level' is our plain reading of B09 and C18, said as such.

### b13 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: creamy layer
**PICTURE:** A report on a desk dated 1980; a government order stamped 1990 with 27%; then a family pyramid with its well-off top shaded out.  
**VOICE:** How did the quota for backward classes come about? The Mandal Commission reported on the last day of nineteen eighty. In nineteen ninety, the Government ordered twenty-seven percent of central civil posts for these classes, in direct recruitment. The Supreme Court's nine judges upheld it in nineteen ninety-two. But the lead judgment set a condition. The creamy layer, the better-off among them, must be left out.  
**SCREEN:** Mandal report: 31 Dec 1980 · 1990 order: 27% of central civil posts · Indra Sawhney (1992), lead judgment: exclude the creamy layer  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-C01 (CORRECTED), POL03-C02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C05 (CONFIRMED)

### b14 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3-4
**PICTURE:** A bar of a year's vacancies with a bright line at the half mark; coloured blocks fill up to it; a new block tries to go above and is stopped.  
**VOICE:** The second limit is the fifty percent rule. A government order of nineteen ninety-three applies it, following that judgment. Reservations for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and backward classes together must not cross half of a year's vacancies. In twenty twenty-one, a five-judge bench kept that ceiling. It said a Maratha quota above it was not justified by exceptional circumstances.  
**SCREEN:** DoPT OM, 1993, following Indra Sawhney: SC + ST + OBC within 50% of a year's vacancies · Jaishri Patil (2021, five judges): ceiling kept  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-C22 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C10 (CONFIRMED)

### b15 · *BUT* · rung 4
**PICTURE:** The vacancy bar again: a new block of 10% sits above the half line. Five judges' chairs: three green, two amber with the word DISSENT.  
**VOICE:** But in twenty nineteen, a new quota went on top. The hundred-and-third Amendment allowed up to ten percent for economically weaker sections. The Supreme Court upheld it by three to two. One of the majority, Justice Maheshwari, wrote about the ceiling. It is not inflexible, he said, and covers only the older classes. Two judges dissented. They held that leaving out the poor among the older groups broke the basic structure. In central jobs, that brings reserved posts to fifty-nine and a half percent, by our arithmetic.  
**SCREEN:** EWS: up to 10%, 103rd Amendment, 2019 · Janhit Abhiyan (2022): upheld 3:2 · 15 + 7.5 + 27 + 10 = 59.5% (our arithmetic)  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-B07 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C12 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C13 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C14 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C24 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Both sides of the split named (dossier section 3).

### b16 · *THEREFORE* · rung 4
**PICTURE:** A tree of the SC list branching into groups, each with a small data chart; a separate scroll labelled ONE JUDGE'S OPINION, three signatures beside it.  
**VOICE:** And the debate goes on. In twenty twenty-four, seven judges ruled six to one. States may sub-classify the Scheduled Castes, if their data shows a group is inadequately represented because of its backwardness. In a separate opinion, one judge said the State must also find a creamy layer among them. That is his opinion, and three judges agreed with it. Reservation in promotion has its own clause too, added in nineteen ninety-five.  
**SCREEN:** Davinder Singh (2024): sub-classification allowed, 6:1 · Gavai J.: creamy layer among SCs (agreed by three judges) · Art. 16(4A), 1995  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-C15 (CONFIRMED), POL03-C16 (CONFIRMED), POL03-B10 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 6

### b17 · *THEREFORE* · rung 5
**PICTURE:** The girl's notebook: the scale balanced; exam lines fill in one by one; the words 'break the promise?' crossed out.  
**VOICE:** So, is reservation a break in the promise of equality? Read plainly, the Constitution's own text says no. It writes reservation into the equality code, for a backward class which, in the State's opinion, is not adequately represented. NCERT says the same. The arguments are about its limits. The creamy layer. The fifty percent ceiling and its exceptions. And the data the State must show. For the exam, write it this way. Article fourteen is equality before law. Fifteen and sixteen bar discrimination, but allow special provisions. Sixteen four makes reservation part of equality, and courts have set its limits.
