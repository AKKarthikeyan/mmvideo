## Chapter 2

### b02 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1
**PICTURE:** Six doors in a row, each opening on its scene: a speaker, a peaceful crowd, a union meeting, a train, a new home, a shop.  
**VOICE:** Article nineteen gives citizens six freedoms. To speak and express yourself. To gather peacefully, without arms. To form associations or unions. To move freely across India. To live and settle anywhere in it. And to practise any profession, trade or business.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 19(1): speech · assembly · associations · movement · residence · profession  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D01 (CONFIRMED)

### b03 · *BUT* · rung 2 · new term: reasonable restrictions
**PICTURE:** Each of the six doors gets a small gate with a sign of its limits; the speech door's sign lists the grounds.  
**VOICE:** But no freedom here is absolute. Each comes with its own limits. Speech can be restricted for the sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, public order, decency or morality. Defamation, contempt of court and incitement to an offence are grounds too. And movement can be limited in the public interest, or to protect a Scheduled Tribe's interests.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 19(2)-(6): reasonable restrictions · speech: sovereignty, security, public order, decency, defamation... · 19(5): Scheduled Tribes  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D03 (CONFIRMED)

### b04 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2-3
**PICTURE:** A phone screen with a post; a law book page labelled 66A tears out and falls away.  
**VOICE:** A restriction must still be reasonable, and courts check that. In twenty fifteen, the Supreme Court struck down Section sixty-six A of the Information Technology Act in full. It held the section violated free speech and was not saved by the allowed limits.  
**SCREEN:** Shreya Singhal (2015): s.66A of the IT Act struck down in full  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D05 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 3

### b05 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A courtroom in warm light; three shields appear in front of the accused, one for each protection.  
**VOICE:** Article twenty protects anyone accused of a crime. You cannot be punished for an act that was not an offence when you did it. You cannot be prosecuted and punished twice for the same offence. And you cannot be forced to be a witness against yourself.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 20: no retroactive offence · no double jeopardy · no compelled self-incrimination  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D07 (CONFIRMED)

### b06 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2-3 · new term: procedure established by law
**PICTURE:** Rights drawn as separate glass boxes on a shelf; a stamped rulebook alone opens the liberty box.  
**VOICE:** Now Article twenty-one. Life and liberty can go only by procedure established by law. In nineteen fifty, in the Gopalan case, a majority of the Court read each right as a separate box. On that reading, a law that set out a procedure could be enough. NCERT's Class Nine glossary says the phrase means strict adherence to duly enacted and valid laws. To our ear, that is the Gopalan idea.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 21 · Gopalan (1950), majority: rights as separate silos · NCERT Grade 9's gloss: the older idea  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D08 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D09 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D11 (CORRECTED)
  
**NOTE:** The steelman: the text, read plainly, sounds like Gopalan.

### b07 · *BUT* · rung 3
**PICTURE:** A passport stamped IMPOUNDED; then the glass boxes melt into one connected web of rights.  
**VOICE:** But in nineteen seventy-eight, Maneka Gandhi's passport was impounded, at first without reasons. Seven judges heard her case. Six joined the lead opinion: the procedure must be right, just and fair, not arbitrary. One judge dissented. It must also pass the test of equality in Article fourteen. And the rights are not separate boxes. Later judges said they overlap.  
**SCREEN:** Maneka Gandhi (1978): procedure must be 'right and just and fair' · tested by Art. 14 · rights overlap (Puttaswamy on Gopalan)  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-L04 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D10 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D09 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** The turn: a valid law is not enough; the procedure must be fair.


## Chapter 4

### b08 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3 · new term: right to privacy
**PICTURE:** A search box over the Constitution's text: 'privacy', 0 results. Then nine chairs on a bench; the Order is signed by all nine.  
**VOICE:** So Article twenty-one grew wider and wider. Take privacy. The word privacy does not appear anywhere in the Constitution. Yet on the twenty-fourth of August, twenty seventeen, nine judges decided it together. Privacy is protected under Article twenty-one, and by the other freedoms.  
**SCREEN:** 'privacy': 0 times in the Constitution · Puttaswamy (24 Aug 2017, nine judges): privacy under Art. 21 and Part III  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-L05 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D12 (CONFIRMED), POL03-L08 (CONFIRMED)

### b09 · *BUT* · rung 4 · new term: proportionality
**PICTURE:** A phone, a bank card and a health app glow; three gates labelled LAW, AIM, PROPORTION stand before them.  
**VOICE:** But privacy is not absolute either. The lead opinion, for four judges, set a three-part test. A law must exist. It must serve a legitimate aim. And the means must be proportionate to that aim. The same opinion said privacy covers your data, with dangers from the State and from others. A data protection law came in twenty twenty-three, and comes into force in stages.  
**SCREEN:** Puttaswamy (lead opinion, 4 judges): legality · legitimate aim · proportionality · informational privacy · DPDP Act 2023, in stages  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D13 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D15 (CONFIRMED), POL03-L03 (CONFIRMED), POL03-X06 (CONFIRMED)

### b10 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3
**PICTURE:** A tree growing from a seed labelled ARTICLE 21: branches for EDUCATION, LIVELIHOOD, WATER AND AIR.  
**VOICE:** Article twenty-one kept widening. Education became its own right in Article twenty-one A, for children aged six to fourteen. NCERT's Class Nine book says it was added in two thousand nine. The amendment is of two thousand two, in force from two thousand ten. The Court also held that the right to life includes livelihood. And it read clean water and air into it.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 21A (86th Amendment 2002, in force 1 Apr 2010; NCERT says 2009) · Olga Tellis (1985): livelihood · Subhash Kumar (1991): water and air  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D16 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D17 (CORRECTED), POL03-D18 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D24 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** NCERT's 2009 is CORRECTED (D17).


## Chapter 5

### b11 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A police station desk: an arrest memo being signed and witnessed; a clock showing 24 hours; a phone call to a relative.  
**VOICE:** What if you are arrested? Article twenty-two says you must be told why, as soon as possible. You may consult a lawyer of your choice. And you must be taken before the nearest magistrate within twenty-four hours. In nineteen ninety-six, the Court added rules for every arrest: a memo of arrest, and a relative or friend told.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 22(1)-(2): grounds · lawyer · magistrate within 24 hours · D.K. Basu (1996): memo of arrest, a relative informed  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D20 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D22 (CONFIRMED)

### b12 · *BUT* · rung 3 · new term: preventive detention
**PICTURE:** A calendar marked at three months with a board of three robed figures; a second calendar marked two months, behind glass labelled NOT IN FORCE.  
**VOICE:** But there is a harder case: preventive detention, to stop a feared act before it happens. The Constitution allows it, with a check. Detention beyond three months needs an Advisory Board's report, unless a law of Parliament provides otherwise. A nineteen seventy-eight amendment would cut that to two months. But in the Legislative Department's text of September twenty twenty-six, that change is still not in force.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 22(4): 3 months, then an Advisory Board · 44th Amendment change to 2 months: 'date to be notified'  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D21 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D21b (CONFIRMED)

### b13 · *THEREFORE* · rung 3-4
**PICTURE:** A courthouse door half-shut by an EMERGENCY bar; two small doors marked 20 and 21 stay open.  
**VOICE:** And during an Emergency? The President may suspend the right to go to court for most Fundamental Rights. But not for Articles twenty and twenty-one. That exception was added in nineteen seventy-eight. In twenty seventeen came Puttaswamy. Its lead opinion, for four judges, overruled the Emergency-era ruling of nineteen seventy-six, ADM Jabalpur.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 359(1): Arts 20 and 21 cannot be suspended (44th Amendment, 1978) · Puttaswamy (2017), lead opinion: ADM Jabalpur (1976) overruled  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-D23 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D14 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 6

### b14 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2-3 · new term: forced labour
**PICTURE:** A stadium under construction in 1982 light; a wage slip below a minimum line; a contractor's office gets the same notice as a government one.  
**VOICE:** Freedom also means freedom from exploitation. Article twenty-three bans trafficking in people, and forced labour. In nineteen eighty-two, workers building for the Asian Games were paid below the minimum wage. The Supreme Court held that this was forced labour. And it held that the Article binds private contractors too.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 23: no trafficking or forced labour · PUDR (1982): below-minimum-wage work = forced labour; binds private contractors  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-E01 (CONFIRMED), POL03-E02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-K07 (CONFIRMED)

### b15 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A school bag replaces a pick at a mine entrance; a sanitation machine replaces a bucket.  
**VOICE:** Article twenty-four bars children under fourteen from factories, mines and other hazardous work. And NCERT Class Eight says a law in force from December twenty thirteen bans employing people as manual scavengers.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 24: no child under 14 in factories, mines, hazardous work · Manual Scavengers Act, in force 6 Dec 2013  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-E03 (CONFIRMED), POL03-E04 (CORRECTED)

**PICTURE:** The prison gate from the start opens onto daylight; the girl's notebook fills with the exam lines.  
**VOICE:** So can any law take your liberty, as long as it follows its own procedure? Since Maneka Gandhi, no. The procedure itself must be fair, just and reasonable, and must pass the test of equality. And since Puttaswamy, a law that touches privacy needs a legitimate aim and proportionate means. That is the lead opinion, for four judges. For the exam, write it this way. Article nineteen gives six freedoms, each with reasonable restrictions. Article twenty protects the accused. Article twenty-one, after Maneka Gandhi, needs fair procedure. It now holds privacy, livelihood, and clean water and air, and Article twenty-one A adds education.
