## Chapter 2

### b02 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1 · new term: freedom of conscience
**PICTURE:** Three verbs written on a notebook page: PROFESS, PRACTISE, PROPAGATE, each with a small generic icon (a voice, a lamp, a book). Generic people of many kinds, no religious dress singled out.  
**VOICE:** Start with religion. Article twenty-five gives every person freedom of conscience. It also gives the right to profess, practise and propagate religion. That means to declare it, follow it and spread it. This right is for all persons, not only citizens.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 25(1): freedom of conscience · profess, practise, propagate · all persons  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-F01 (CONFIRMED)

### b03 · *BUT* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** The three verbs sit inside a frame whose edges are labelled PUBLIC ORDER, MORALITY, HEALTH. A small gear marked SECULAR ACTIVITY turns at the side. NCERT's three examples appear only as words on a crossed-out card, never as pictures.  
**VOICE:** But this freedom has limits written into it. It is subject to public order, morality and health. The State may also regulate economic, financial, political or other worldly activity linked to religious practice. It may pass laws for social welfare and reform. NCERT gives sati, bigamy and human sacrifice as practices the State may stop. And NCERT says spreading your faith is allowed, but forcing anyone to convert is not.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 25(1)-(2): public order, morality, health · secular activity · social reform · NCERT: no forcible conversion  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-F02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-F07 (CONFIRMED)

### b04 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2-3
**PICTURE:** The school gate from the opening reopens; the three children walk back in. A card: ARTS 19(1)(a) + 25(1).  
**VOICE:** So back to the anthem. The children had stood in respect. They had disturbed no one. The Supreme Court held that expelling them broke two rights. Freedom of speech and expression under Article nineteen, and freedom of religion under Article twenty-five. As the Court put it, no law makes anyone sing the anthem.  
**SCREEN:** Bijoe Emmanuel (1986): Arts 19(1)(a) and 25(1) infringed  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-F06 (CONFIRMED)

### b05 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A generic temple building with open doors and many people walking in; inside, a small reserved space for the denomination's own ritual. Balance icon: ART. 25(2)(b) and ART. 26(b), both lit.  
**VOICE:** Article twenty-six is for groups. Every religious denomination may set up its own institutions and manage its own religious affairs. It may own property and run it as the law allows. In nineteen fifty-seven, this clashed with a law opening public temples to all Hindus. The Court harmonised the two. The temple had to open to the Hindu public. The denomination kept some of its own rites.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 26: denominations' own affairs · Venkataramana Devaru (1957): temple open to the Hindu public, own rites kept  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-F03 (CONFIRMED), POL03-F05 (CONFIRMED), POL03-F02 (CONFIRMED)

### b06 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A tax receipt with a blank 'religion' line; a State school whose notice board reads ALL WELCOME; the Preamble page with the word SECULAR written in.  
**VOICE:** Two more rules keep the State neutral. No one can be made to pay a tax spent on promoting one particular religion. And a school wholly maintained from State funds cannot give religious instruction. The word secular was added to the Preamble by the nineteen seventy-six amendment, in force from January nineteen seventy-seven. NCERT, writing on equal treatment of all religions, says it plainly. India has no official religion.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 27: no tax for one religion · Art. 28(1): no religious instruction in wholly State-funded schools · 'secular' in Preamble (42nd Amendment, in force 1977)  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-F04 (CONFIRMED), POL03-F08 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 3

### b07 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1
**PICTURE:** Many scripts written on a long ribbon across the screen, generic and varied; a school door with an open sign.  
**VOICE:** Now culture and education. Article twenty-nine says any section of citizens with its own language, script or culture may conserve it. Notice the words, any section. So the text itself does not limit it to minorities, though the article's heading speaks of them. And no State-run or State-aided school may refuse a citizen only for religion, race, caste or language.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 29(1): any section may conserve its language, script, culture · 29(2): no denial of admission  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-G01 (CONFIRMED)

### b08 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A plain school building with a nameplate MANAGED BY A MINORITY; an aid envelope reaches it like any other school. A dictionary page titled MINORITY in the Constitution is blank.  
**VOICE:** Article thirty goes further for minorities. Religious and linguistic minorities may set up and run schools and colleges of their choice. The State cannot refuse aid to a school just because a minority manages it. Yet the Constitution never defines who counts as a minority. NCERT describes a group outnumbered in a part of the country or the whole.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 30(1)-(2): minorities' own institutions; no discrimination in aid · 'minority' not defined in the text  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-G02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-G03 (CONFIRMED)

### b09 · *BUT* · rung 3
**PICTURE:** Two speech bubbles of the same size and colour, side by side, with no faces; under them, the Article 29 and 30 cards.  
**VOICE:** But NCERT itself notes a debate. NCERT Class Eight says the two aims can seem to clash. It says some people feel minority protections may go against secularism. Here is our reading of the text. Article twenty-nine bars denial of admission on religion, race, caste or language. Article thirty lets minorities run their own institutions. Both are in the Constitution.  
**SCREEN:** NCERT: 'some people feel' minority protections may go against secularism · the text: Arts 29-30  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-F09 (CONFIRMED), POL03-G01 (CONFIRMED), POL03-G02 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Both sides shown equally; NCERT's framing only.


## Chapter 4

### b10 · *THEREFORE* · rung 1 · new term: writ
**PICTURE:** A courthouse door labelled ART. 32 with a key in it; a smaller door ART. 226 beside it. A robed, faceless silhouette at a lectern with a name plaque B.R. AMBEDKAR, 1948, and the quote written beside it.  
**VOICE:** A right is only as strong as the way to enforce it. So Article thirty-two makes going to the Supreme Court a right in itself. The Court can issue orders and writs to enforce your rights. Under Article two hundred twenty-six, every High Court can too. Ambedkar told the Constituent Assembly it was the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 32: moving the Supreme Court is a right · Art. 226: High Courts · Ambedkar (9 Dec 1948): 'soul' and 'heart'  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-H01 (CONFIRMED), POL03-H02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-H03 (CONFIRMED)

### b11 · *BUT* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** The rights shelf from Part 2, with a fence at its edge labelled ARTS 33-34, and a sealed box labelled NINTH SCHEDULE set aside with a tag 'POL-06'.  
**VOICE:** But Part Three has its own outer limits. Parliament decides how far these rights apply to the armed forces. It can also protect acts done to restore order where martial law was in force. And some land and policy laws are shielded from challenge on some of these rights. Laws in the Ninth Schedule, as the text reads, on all of them. We will open that box in the basic structure episode.  
**SCREEN:** Arts 33-34: armed forces, martial law · Arts 31A, 31B (Ninth Schedule), 31C: shielded laws (see POL-06)  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-H04 (CONFIRMED), POL03-H05 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 5

### b12 · *BUT* · rung turn · new term: Directive Principles
**PICTURE:** The PART IV book opens into a compass; its needle points to a horizon marked GOALS. No gavel on this side.  
**VOICE:** But rights are not the whole book. Now Part Four, the Directive Principles. Article thirty-seven says no court can enforce them. Yet it calls them fundamental in the governance of the country. The State has a duty to apply them when it makes laws. So they are not rights you can sue for. They are goals the State has promised to work towards.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 37: not enforceable by any court · fundamental in governance · State's duty in making laws  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-I01 (CONFIRMED)

### b13 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A horizon of small signposts, one per goal, each with its article number, appearing one by one as they are named; the text only, no scene for Article 44.  
**VOICE:** The goals are broad. A livelihood for men and women, and equal pay for equal work. Resources shared for the common good. Village panchayats with the powers to govern themselves. The right to work and to public help in old age, sickness and unemployment, within the State's means. A living wage. Care and education for every child below six. Judges kept separate from the executive in the State's public services. And a uniform civil code, and better nutrition and public health.  
**SCREEN:** Arts 38-39 livelihood, equal pay · 40 panchayats · 41 work, assistance · 43 living wage · 44 uniform civil code · 45 early childhood · 47 nutrition · 50 judiciary separate  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-I02 (CONFIRMED), POL03-I03 (CONFIRMED), POL03-I03b (CONFIRMED), POL03-I03c (CONFIRMED), POL03-I05 (CONFIRMED), POL03-I04 (CONFIRMED)
  
**NOTE:** Art. 44 read as text, not argued (dossier section 5).


## Chapter 6

### b14 · *BUT* · rung 3
**PICTURE:** The two books from the opening collide; a land map between them tears. A card PROPERTY moves from the PART III shelf to a separate plinth labelled ART. 300A.  
**VOICE:** But these goals ran into rights. NCERT says laws to abolish the zamindari system were opposed as violating the right to property. A long legal battle followed. NCERT says the court held that Fundamental Rights were so sacred they could not be limited even for these goals. Property itself was a fundamental right until June nineteen seventy-nine. Since then it is no longer a fundamental right. Article three hundred A, in another part of the Constitution, protects it. The State can take your property only by authority of law.  
**SCREEN:** land reform vs property · Arts 19(1)(f), 31 omitted (in force 20 Jun 1979) · Art. 300A: only by authority of law  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-I06 (CONFIRMED), POL03-J01 (CONFIRMED), POL03-D04 (CONFIRMED)

### b15 · *THEREFORE* · rung 5
**PICTURE:** The brass balance from Part 1 returns, PART III in one pan and PART IV in the other, level. Nine empty judges' chairs, labelled by count only; a card ART. 31C (AS UPHELD IN KESAVANANDA) stays, a card ART. 31C (1976) is crossed out.  
**VOICE:** So which one wins? Neither wins outright. The Minerva Mills case was decided in nineteen eighty. The majority held that the balance between Part Three and Part Four is itself a basic feature. Neither can be given absolute primacy over the other. In twenty twenty-four, nine judges held that Article thirty-one C, as the Kesavananda Bharati case upheld it, still stands. The wider version from nineteen seventy-six had been struck down in Minerva Mills.  
**SCREEN:** Minerva Mills (1980): FR-DPSP balance is a basic feature · Property Owners Association (2024): Art. 31C as upheld in Kesavananda in force  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-I06 (CONFIRMED), POL03-H06 (CONFIRMED)


## Chapter 7

### b16 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** The girl's notebook: a page titled DUTIES with eleven short lines; the eleventh line is a school bag. A small note at the bottom: RIGHTS DO NOT WAIT FOR DUTIES (NCERT).  
**VOICE:** Rights also come with duties. Article fifty-one A lists eleven Fundamental Duties. Ten came with the nineteen seventy-six amendment, in force from January nineteen seventy-seven. The eleventh was added later. Parents and guardians must give a child aged six to fourteen the chance to study. No clause lets a court enforce these duties. And NCERT says your rights do not depend on doing them first.  
**SCREEN:** Art. 51A: 11 duties · 10 from 42nd Amendment (1976, in force 1977) · 11th: education, ages 6-14 (86th) · not court-enforceable  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-I07 (CONFIRMED), POL03-K17 (CONFIRMED), POL03-I08 (CONFIRMED)

### b17 · *THEREFORE* · rung 2
**PICTURE:** A plain office door labelled NATIONAL HUMAN RIGHTS COMMISSION; a recommendation letter goes out, no gavel.  
**VOICE:** And when a public servant violates human rights, the National Human Rights Commission can step in. It was set up under the Protection of Human Rights Act, nineteen ninety-three. It can inquire and recommend. It cannot punish anyone itself.  
**SCREEN:** NHRC: Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 · recommends, does not prosecute  
**CLAIMS:** POL03-H07 (CORRECTED)


## Chapter 8

### b18 · *THEREFORE* · rung 5
**PICTURE:** The balance stays level; the girl's notebook fills with the exam lines; the three-stop path is complete.  
**VOICE:** So, rights you can enforce, and goals you cannot. When they clash, neither simply wins. The Supreme Court has held that the balance between them is a basic feature. For the exam, write it this way. Articles twenty-five to twenty-eight give freedom of religion, within public order, morality and health. Articles twenty-nine and thirty protect culture and minority education. Article thirty-two makes the remedy a right. The Directive Principles guide the State but cannot be enforced. In Minerva Mills the majority held their balance with rights a basic feature. Property is no longer a fundamental right; Article three hundred A protects it. And there are eleven duties.  
**SCREEN:** exam card: Arts 25-28 · 29-30 · 32 · Part IV (Art. 37) · Minerva Mills balance · Art. 300A · 11 duties
