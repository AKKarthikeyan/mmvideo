# POL-06 dossier: "Basic Structure: the case that limited Parliament"

Research only, 2 Nov 2026. Claim ids (POL06-xxx) point to `claims.csv`. Judgments other than Kesavananda were read on Indian Kanoon copies of the Supreme Court Reports text, because the court's own search needs a captcha; each such claim says so.

## 1. What the topic is, and the order to meet its ideas

The question is simple: **can Parliament change any part of the Constitution, including the fundamental rights?** Learners should meet it in this order.

1. **Article 368 is the amending article.** Parliament may amend "by way of addition, variation or repeal" (368(1)); a Bill needs a majority of the House's total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting, in each House; some subjects also need half the States; the President must assent (A01-A03). NCERT adds that some provisions change like ordinary laws (Arts 2, 3).
2. **The first fight: is an amendment "law" under Article 13(2)?** If yes, it cannot take away fundamental rights. *Shankari Prasad* (1951, five judges, unanimous) said no (C01); *Sajjan Singh* (1964) agreed, three judges to two on reasoning, with Mudholkar J. raising the first doubt about "basic features" (C02-C03); *Golaknath* (1967, eleven judges, 6:5) said yes and froze Part III for the future (C04).
3. **Parliament answers.** The 24th Amendment (1971) inserted Art. 13(4) and 368(1),(3) to make the power explicit (A04, B01); the 25th and 29th Amendments widened property and Ninth Schedule protection (B02-B03).
4. **Kesavananda Bharati (24 Apr 1973, thirteen judges, eleven judgments).** The petitioner lost on the amendments (24th, 25th s.2, 29th valid) but the Court held, by 7 to 6, that "amendment" does not include power to alter the basic structure or framework (D04-D08).
5. **What counts as basic structure?** There is no authoritative list; every judge gave different illustrations. Bhagwati J.'s separate opinion in Minerva Mills later said they bind nobody, but Goel J. in NJAC (para 15.1) counts them as illustrations by the majority of seven, so the Court itself is not agreed (E01-E06, E06 CONFLICT).
6. **How it was used.** 42nd Amendment (1976) tried to remove the limit; Minerva Mills (1980) struck it down (B05, F04-F05). In Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975) the five judges struck down or held unable to stand clause (4) of Art. 329A on different grounds, and only Chandrachud J. also names clause (5); never say the Court unanimously struck down both. Waman Rao (1980) and Coelho (2007) applied the doctrine to the Ninth Schedule (F01, F06-F07). NJAC (2015) used it for judicial independence (F08).

## 2. The hard parts and the common mistakes (correct position, with sources)

- **"7-6" is only one count.** Seven judges hold the basic-structure limit; ten overruled Golaknath's Art. 13(2) view; seven hold the amending power plenary; nine signed the summary. Khanna J. is in the seven on the basic-structure limit and also in the seven on "no implied limits" (D04-D09). Say "7:6 on this question", never "a 7-6 verdict".
- **The signed summary is itself disputed.** Chandrachud CJ (Minerva Mills) accepts item 2 as the majority view; Bhagwati J. says the summary has no legal effect (D10, CONFLICT). Two signers (Palekar, Chandrachud) are shown by the headnote as dissenting on the basic-structure point (D09).
- **"The Court gave a list."** False. Each judge gave illustrations; Bhagwati J.'s separate opinion in Minerva Mills (not the majority) says they are non-binding and each case is decided on the feature's place in the scheme, its object and the effect of removing it, while Goel J. in NJAC counts them as illustrations by the majority (E06, CONFLICT). NCERT sets the statement for the student to judge; our reading is that there is no single official list (E07).
- **"Parliament cannot amend fundamental rights."** That was Golaknath, overruled in 1973. Under Kesavananda any provision, including Part III, can be amended, except to destroy the basic structure (D05, F07). The Constitution still contains Art. 368(4)-(5) printed, but the Legislative Department's footnote marks them invalid (A05).
- **"Basic structure is in the Constitution."** Searched the whole 10 Sep 2026 text: zero hits (A06). It rests on the meaning of the word "amendment".
- **"Applies to all laws."** No: it limits constitutional amendments; ordinary laws are tested against the Constitution (F02).
- **"Indira Gandhi was the first amendment struck down."** Not safe: Kesavananda already struck the second part of s.3 of the 25th Amendment (F03, CORRECTED).
- **Property.** NCERT Class XI ch.6 says the Court held the right to property not basic; the judgment does not say so as a Court holding (H06, CONFLICT).

## 3. Debates and who holds which view (from the judges' own words)

- **Parliamentary sovereignty vs constitutional supremacy.** Ray J.'s headnote summary says every provision is essential and no line can be drawn between essential and other features; the other five dissenters (Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi, Chandrachud) held the power plenary (D04, D06); their full reasoning was not read. Sikri, Shelat, Grover, Hegde, Mukherjea, Reddy, Khanna: "amendment" implies keeping the Constitution's identity (D04, E01-E05).
- **Who speaks for the people?** Hegde J. says a two-thirds Parliament need not represent a majority of voters; Ambedkar (25 Nov 1949) said inability to get two-thirds shows dissatisfaction is not general (G04).
- **Fixed list vs case-by-case.** Ray CJ called finding essential features an exercise in imponderables; Hegde J. said it is no harder than other judicial tests; Bhagwati J. in Minerva Mills, citing Chandrachud J. in Indira Gandhi, favours case-by-case (E06). This is a separate opinion, not a majority holding.
- **NJAC, 4:1.** Chelameswar J. would have upheld the 99th Amendment (F08).
- Scholars (Andhyarujina, Baxi, Dhavan, Minattur and others) are named on the Court's reference list but were **not read**; no scholar view is stated here.
