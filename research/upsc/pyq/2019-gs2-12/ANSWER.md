# PYQ-2019-GS2-12: Can Parliament Amend Its Way Past the Basic Structure? (UPSC GS II 2019)

**Question** (UPSC CSE (Main) 2019, General Studies Paper II, Q12, 15 marks):
> “Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution is a limited power and it cannot be enlarged into absolute power.” In the light of this statement explain whether Parliament under Article 368 of the Constitution can destroy the Basic Structure of the Constitution by expanding its amending power ?

Checked against the PDF: research/upsc/_pyq/mains/2019_gs2.pdf page 3.

**Working budget:** 220-250 words, about 11 minutes in the hall (UPSC's printed limit is 250 words (P005); 11 minutes is our arithmetic from 3 hours for 250 marks). Sheet: 236 words.

## Demands

- **D1** Explain the statement: why the amending power is limited and cannot be made absolute
- **D2** What Article 368 lets Parliament do, and how
- **D3** Whether Parliament can destroy the basic structure by expanding the power (the 42nd Amendment and the cases)
- **D4** Weigh it: what Parliament can still change, and why the limit exists

## The answer sheet

The statement echoes _Minerva Mills_ (1980): a _limited amending power_ is itself a basic feature, so Parliament cannot use it to make itself all-powerful. _(demands D1; claims POL06-F05, P006)_

**1. The power under Article 368** _(demands D2; claims POL06-A01)_

- Parliament may amend 'by addition, variation or repeal any provision' (Art. 368(1)).
- _Special majority_: a majority of each House's total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting; some federal provisions also need half the States.
- Art. 368(3): Art. 13 does not apply to amendments. _(demands D2; claims POL06-A01, POL06-A02, POL06-A03, POL06-A04)_

**2. Can it destroy the basic structure? No** _(demands D3; claims )_

- _Kesavananda Bharati_ (1973, 13 judges, 7:6): the amending power does not include the power to alter the _basic structure_.
- The 42nd Amendment (1976) tried to expand it: _Art. 368(4)_ barred courts from questioning any amendment, and 368(5) declared 'no limitation whatever'.
- Minerva Mills (1980) struck both down 5:0: 'The power to destroy is not a power to amend.'
- The test still bites: the 99th Amendment (_NJAC_) fell 4:1 in 2015; I.R. Coelho (2007) opened post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws to review. _(demands D3, D1; claims POL06-D01, POL06-D04, POL06-B05, P004, POL06-F04, P006, POL06-F08, POL06-F07)_

[Diagram: Fig. 1: The limit, and the attempt to remove it] A timeline arrow: Art. 368 power → Kesavananda sets the limit (1973) → the 42nd Amendment adds 368(4)-(5) (1976) → Minerva Mills strikes them down (1980) → NJAC (2015). _(demands D3; claims POL06-D01, POL06-B05, POL06-F04, POL06-F08, POL06-A01)_

**3. Weighing it** _(demands D4; claims )_

- Parliament can still amend any part within the limit; the _judiciary_ decides whether it is crossed (NCERT).
- What counts as basic is still debated.
- Change stays open: Ambedkar held that those who cannot win a two-thirds majority cannot claim the public's backing. _(demands D4; claims POL06-H01, POL06-E07, POL06-G04)_

So Parliament can amend the Constitution but not unmake it: the limit guards its identity while keeping it open to change. _(demands D1, D3, D4; claims POL06-D04)_

## The weaker answer (ours, for comparison)

- The basic structure is laid down in Article 368. → **the words 'basic structure' appear nowhere in the Constitution**
- Kesavananda Bharati gave a fixed list of the basic features. → **NCERT itself says what counts as basic is still debated**
- Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain was the first time an amendment was struck down. → **a common belief the record does not support**
- So Parliament can never amend Fundamental Rights. → **wrong: it can amend any part within the limit**
- (no mention of Article 368(4) and (5)) → **skips the question's own words: 'by expanding its amending power'**
