## The promise
Every object you use without thinking had a first day, a person, and a trick of physics behind it. Each episode tells
both, in under 90 seconds, with the original patent or paper on screen and India's own story where it is true.

## Episode format (75-90 s)
| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| HOOK | One surprising, documented line, on screen from the first frame. |
| BEFORE IT EXISTED | How people managed (punkah, Himalayan ice, clay pans). |
| THE INVENTION | Dated moments with names and places; the original patent drawing or paper page on screen with its number. |
| HOW IT WORKS | The science in three plain steps, one diagram building up. |

| INDIA TODAY | One official number (NFHS household ownership, AIR coverage, BEE rules). |
| SIGN-OFF | "Now you know." |

**Visual signature:** the original patent drawing (US/GB/DE patents before 1931 are public domain), sliding in like a
document with its number stamped, then redrawn as our own animated diagram. Everything else drawn in code in the pilot's
look. Never CC BY-SA images; Science Museum Group images are CC BY-NC-SA, so no.

**Accuracy rules:** primary sources only for on-screen facts (patents, IEEE Milestones, museum records, official Indian
data); contested history said precisely ("first public demonstration", "patented the idea", "Godrej says"); company claims
attributed; myths named only to correct them.

## Season 1: the home (AK's five)
Suggested order: radio (strongest India hook), fridge, fan, TV, AC. Facts below were checked 22 Oct 2026 against patents,
IEEE Milestones, museum records and official Indian sources; each episode's "don't say" list holds the myths found.

### 1. Radio
- **Hook:** "In 1895, in a Kolkata college, a professor rang a bell and set off gunpowder in another room, with no wire,
  through two walls."
- **Before:** messages travelled by wire (telegraph) or by hand.
- **Invention:** Maxwell predicts electromagnetic waves (1865); Hertz makes and detects them in Karlsruhe (1886-88);
  J.C. Bose sends 60 GHz waves 23 m through two walls at Presidency College, Calcutta (1895, IEEE Milestone 2012);
  Marconi patents wireless telegraphy (GB 12039, 1896; US 586,193, 1897) and reports hearing "S" across the Atlantic
  (12 Dec 1901); Nobel Prize 1909 shared by Marconi and Braun.
- **How it works:** (1) the transmitter pushes electrons back and forth in an antenna, which sends out a wave; (2) the wave
  crosses space at the speed of light and nudges electrons in your radio's antenna; (3) tuning picks one frequency (AM changes
  the wave's strength, FM its frequency).
- **Nobody tells you:** "who invented radio" has no single answer (Hertz, Bose, Marconi, Braun, Popov, Lodge all have parts);
  and the 1943 US Supreme Court case did not rule that Tesla invented radio: it found Marconi's tuning patent anticipated by
  earlier patents, including Tesla's.
- **India today:** first broadcasts by the Radio Club of Bombay (June 1923); All India Radio named 8 Jun 1936; 6 stations at
  independence covering 11% of people, today about 99% (Prasar Bharati). Homes with a radio set: 30.9% (2005-06) → 4.9%
  (2019-21) (NFHS).
- **Patent visuals:** US 586,193 (Marconi), US 755,840 (Bose's galena detector, 1904), US 763,772.
- **Don't say:** "Bose invented radio before Marconi"; "Marconi stole Bose's coherer" (say "a detector of a type Bose described
  in 1899; the Science Museum says it may be the one used"); "Marconi proved transatlantic radio" (say "reported hearing");
  "Maxwell's four equations" (Heaviside's form); Fessenden's Christmas Eve 1906 broadcast as certain.

### 2. Refrigerator
- **Hook:** "In May 1833 a ship left Boston with 180 tons of frozen lake ice for Calcutta. Four months later, about 100 tons
  were still ice."
- **Before:** Akbar's court chilled water with saltpetre and brought ice from the mountains near Lahore (Ain-i-Akbari, 1590s);
  in 1775 a British officer described ice made overnight in porous clay pans at Allahabad (Phil. Trans.).
- **Invention:** William Cullen freezes water by evaporating ether in Glasgow (read 1 May 1755, printed 1756); Jacob Perkins
  patents the first working vapour-compression machine (GB 6662, 1834); DOMELRE, first mass-marketed electric unit (1913);
  Midgley's fluorine refrigerants (US 1,833,847, 1931); Einstein and Szilard patent a fridge with no compressor
  (US 1,781,541, 1930); ozone damage found (1974, 1985) → Montreal Protocol (16 Sep 1987).
- **How it works:** (1) inside, a liquid boils into gas in thin pipes and soaks up heat (Cullen's 1755 trick); (2) the
  compressor squeezes the gas and makes it hotter than your kitchen; (3) coils at the back give that heat to the room and it
  turns back into liquid. A fridge doesn't make cold: it moves heat, which is why the back is warm.
- **Nobody tells you:** Einstein co-owns a fridge patent; the "he did it after a family died from a leak" story has no
  contemporary record. Leaving the door open warms your kitchen.
- **India today:** 37.9% of households have a fridge (urban 63.4%, rural 25.2%), up from 29.6% in 2015-16 (NFHS). Godrej says
  it made the first Indian refrigerator in 1958.
- **Patent visuals:** US 1,781,541 (Einstein-Szilard), US 1,833,847 (Midgley); Perkins machine drawing (Scientific American,
  1883, public domain).
- **Don't say:** Cullen 1748; "Perkins invented the fridge"; "Tudor brought India its first ice"; "Monitor Top was the first
  home fridge"; Einstein's fridge "had no moving parts" (the patent says no compressor); Godrej "first" without "Godrej says";
  any BEE date for direct-cool fridges (not confirmed).

### 3. Electric fan
- **Hook:** "A ceiling fan cools you and nothing else. Leave it running in an empty room and the room doesn't get one degree
  cooler."
- **Before:** the punkah, a cloth frame swung by a servant (the punkah-wallah). In 1810 a visitor wrote that dinner in India
  was "scarcely possible" without one (Maria Graham, quoted in Hobson-Jobson, 1886).
- **Invention:** Edison company fan motor c.1885 (Smithsonian); Schuyler Wheeler credited with an early electric fan (date
  disputed: 1882 or 1886; Franklin Institute medal for it, 1904); Philip Diehl patents a ceiling-hung "electric-motor fan"
  (US 414,758, 12 Nov 1889); Tesla's rotating-field induction motor patent (US 381,968, 1888) is the idea inside ordinary
  fans; by the 1890s floor, wall, ceiling and desk fans were all on sale (Smithsonian).
- **How it works:** (1) current through copper coils makes magnets that push and pull the rotor round (induction motor; new
  BLDC fans use a brushless DC motor); (2) the tilted blades act like small rotating wings and throw air down (NASA Glenn);
  (3) moving air carries heat and sweat vapour off your skin, so sweat evaporates faster: you feel cooler, the air doesn't get
  colder (US DOE: "fans cool people, not rooms").
- **Nobody tells you:** the punkah isn't an ancient Indian invention: swinging-frame fans were known to the Arabs by the 8th
  century, and nobody knows when it reached India (Hobson-Jobson). And switch the fan off when you leave the room.
- **India today:** 88.3% of households have an electric fan (urban 96.4%, rural 84.3%; NFHS-5), the most-owned appliance in the
  survey. India's fan stock is 400-500 million, heading to 900-1,000 million by 2037-38; an ordinary fan uses about 70 W, a
  star-rated one about 50 W, BLDC about 35 W (India Cooling Action Plan, 2019).
- **Patent visuals:** US 414,758 / 414,757 / 417,474 / 550,042 (Diehl), US 381,968 (Tesla); punkah drawings: British Library
  WD 2904 (1863) and D'Oyly's "A Punca Bearer" (Yale, CC0).
- **Don't say:** "Wheeler patented the fan in 1882"; Diehl "invented the first ceiling fan" (say "an early"); US 373,391 (that's a
  shoe-sewing patent); "88% have a ceiling fan" (NFHS says electric fan); "fans always help in extreme heat" (studies disagree
  above 35 °C); a date for mandatory BEE fan labels (sources conflict: May 2022 vs 1 Jan 2023).

### 4. Television
- **Hook:** "In 2005, about 31 of every 100 Indian homes had a radio and 44 had a TV. Fifteen years later: 5 and 68."
- **Invention:** Paul Nipkow patents a spinning scanning disc (DE 30105, 1884); John Logie Baird gives the first public
  demonstration of live TV in London (26 Jan 1926, IEEE); Kenjiro Takayanagi shows a 40-line picture on a cathode-ray tube
  (25 Dec 1926); Philo Farnsworth files his electronic TV patent (Jan 1927, US 1,773,980, granted 1930); Zworykin's
  application (1923) is granted in 1938 (US 2,141,059).
- **How it works:** (1) the camera breaks the picture into lines and dots and turns each dot's brightness into a signal;
  (2) the signal travels by cable or radio wave; (3) the TV redraws the same lines in the same order, many times a second
  (India's old standard: 625 lines, 25 pictures a second), so fast that your brain sees smooth motion.
- **Nobody tells you:** "persistence of vision" is a myth; the accepted explanation is apparent motion. And "LED TV" is
  usually an LCD screen with LED lights behind it.
- **India today:** Doordarshan began on 15 Sep 1959 with equipment a European company gifted after an expo; regular service
  1965; first colour telecast 15 Aug 1982 (I&B Ministry). 67.8% of households own a TV (NFHS-5).
- **Patent visuals:** DE 30105 (Nipkow), GB 222,604 (Baird), US 1,773,980 (Farnsworth).
- **Don't say:** "Baird invented TV"; the Farnsworth-RCA court details or $1 million payment (not yet primary-sourced); a frame
  rate for Baird's 1926 demonstration.

### 5. Air conditioner
- **Hook:** "The first modern air conditioner wasn't built to cool people. It was built in 1902 so a printing plant's
  coloured inks would line up when humid summer air made the paper swell."
- **Before:** in 1851 a Florida doctor, John Gorrie, patented an ice-making machine (US 8,080) and used ice and cooled air to
  treat fever patients (Smithsonian).
- **Invention:** Willis Carrier's system for the Sackett & Wilhelms printing plant, Brooklyn (1902, humidity held at 55%);
  New York Stock Exchange (1903), the first high-profile cooling for comfort (ASHRAE); Carrier's humidity-and-temperature
  control patent (US 1,085,971, 1914, for textile mills); first known air-conditioned cinema (New Empire Theater, Alabama,
  1917); Carrier's centrifugal compressor shown in 1922, in a Detroit store and a Dallas theatre by 1924 (Smithsonian).
- **How it works (US DOE):** (1) an AC is a fridge for your room: it moves heat out, it doesn't make cold; (2) indoors, the
  refrigerant boils in the cold coil and soaks up the room's heat, pushed round by a compressor; (3) outdoors, the hot gas
  gives that heat to the outside air and turns back to liquid. That's why the outdoor unit blows hot air.
- **Nobody tells you:** Carrier's famous 1906 patent (US 808,897) is an air washer: the patent says the cooling coils are "no
  part of the invention". And the phrase "air conditioning" is credited to textile engineer Stuart Cramer, not Carrier.
- **India today:** NFHS-5 counts "air conditioner/cooler" together: 23.7% of households (urban 39.5%, rural 15.8%). True ACs
  were about 8% of homes in 2017-18, projected 40% by 2037-38 (India Cooling Action Plan). Since 1 Jan 2020 every star-labelled
  AC comes set to 24 °C by default; you can change it, and BEE estimates about 6% power saved per degree higher (Ministry of
  Power/PIB).
- **Patent visuals:** US 8,080 (Gorrie) plus the Smithsonian's Gorrie patent model photo (CC0); US 808,897 and US 1,085,971
  (Carrier).
- **Don't say:** "Carrier invented AC to cool people"; "the 1906 patent is the AC patent"; "Carrier coined air conditioning";
  "Gorrie invented AC"; "the Rivoli was the first AC cinema"; "23.7% of homes have an AC" (that line includes coolers);
  "24 °C is a limit" (it's the default); the 20-28 °C range (announced June 2025, not confirmed as in force).
