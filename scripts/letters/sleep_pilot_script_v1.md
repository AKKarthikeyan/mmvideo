# SLEEP PILOT — script v1 (DRAFT, awaiting AK approval; no production started)
Working title: "The Light Bulb Problem"
Source: Nomad Investment Partnership letters (Sleep, Zakaria and Company), compiled PDF on the Mini
  (iCloud Downloads/"Full Collection Nomad Letters Nick Sleep.pdf"). All on-screen quotes verbatim, checked by pdftotext 29 Sep 2026.
Structure: Nolan-style "Pledge / Turn / Prestige" with a cold open from the middle, a time-fold, and a loop back to the open.
Runtime target ~6:30, 16:9 long-form + one 55s vertical (the light bulb).

BEAT 0  COLD OPEN (0:00-0:30)  [black, a slow tick, letter page 147 table fades in, circle on -45.3%]
VO: December 2008. A fund manager in London sits down to write to his investors. His fund has just lost forty-five point three percent in a year. The world index lost forty point seven. It is the worst year in the fund's table.
VO: And in that same letter he writes a sentence about somebody else's business. Not his own. "For these high street competitors the game is over."
VO: His name is Nick Sleep. Watch closely. Nothing is hidden. It just isn't where you are looking.
ON SCREEN: Nomad letter, period ended 31 Dec 2008, p.147 table (-45.3% vs -40.7%) ; then p.151 line highlighted.

BEAT 1  THE PLEDGE (0:30-1:45)  [two shopfronts, split screen]
VO: Two shops. The first plays the old game. Prices are high, so every week something goes on sale to pull you through the door. Sleep's verdict is short.
QUOTE: "customers are trained to buy on deal, be disloyal and shop around" ... "Yuck."  (p.151)
VO: The second shop does something that looks like a mistake. Every time it gets more efficient, it does not keep the saving. It hands it to you.
QUOTE: "As the firm grows in size, scale savings are given back to the customer in the form of lower prices."  (p.151)
VO: You buy more. The shop gets bigger. It gets cheaper to run. And the saving goes out the door again. A loop. Sleep called the result "Yippee".
VO: This is why, he wrote, Costco sells four times more per foot of shop floor than a run-of-the-mill supermarket.
VISUAL: the loop drawn as a spiral that tightens; tick sound locks to each turn.

BEAT 2  THE TURN (1:45-3:00)  [arithmetic on a chalk/paper ledger]
VO: Here is the part that should not be possible. Take Amazon's operating costs per dollar of sales, and add its operating margin.
QUOTE: "less than some of its high street peers' costs (per dollar of sales)."  (p.151)
VO: Read that again. Amazon's costs plus its profit are lower than what those shops merely spend. So a rival could sell at zero profit and still not undercut Amazon's price. That is the game being over.
VO: Sleep estimated that Amazon's nearest high-street rivals had combined US revenues of a hundred and fifty billion dollars. He named three that had already gone: Circuit City, Woolworths, Zavvi.  (p.152)
VISUAL: two stacked bars: rival cost bar taller than Amazon cost+margin bar. Rival bar is stamped "no way to win".

BEAT 3  THE FOLD - "FORTY YEARS EARLIER" (3:00-4:15)  [calendar pages run backwards, tick reverses]
VO: But Sleep did not discover this in 2008. He kept going back further. To the shop his letters keep coming back to. Wal-Mart.
VO: For forty years, he says, investors sat on conference calls and cheered.
QUOTE: "great quarter, Wal-Mart"  (p.162)
VO: They saw the information. In his words, they "incorrectly weigh the information." They watched valuation, margin trends, revenue growth.
QUOTE: "the central engine of success at Wal-Mart was a thrift orientation fueling growth with the savings shared with the customer."  (p.162)
VO: The numbers moved every quarter. The engine never did.
QUOTE: "average companies are more like GM than Wal-Mart!"  (p.163)

BEAT 4  THE SECOND QUESTION (4:15-5:30)  [a dark room; a single light bulb]
VO: A fair objection. Why can't a rival simply copy it? Sleep asks the same thing, and his answer starts with a confession. He had been looking for a vivid smoking gun.
QUOTE: "a brand name, a location, a clever re-insurance contract, or a patent."  (p.174)
VO: One big thing. And one big thing is fragile. A drug company with a patent can be displaced overnight.
QUOTE: "A rival could displace it at any time with a better chemical"  (p.175)
VO: A shop running the loop is different. To beat its costs, a rival would need to be better at not one thing but...
QUOTE: "a million little actions"  (p.175)
VO: Amazon's shareholder letter that year: four hundred and fifty-two detailed goals, each with an owner and a date. And one employee idea, from Sleep's own letter: remove the light bulbs from the vending machines. Really. It saves the firm twenty thousand dollars a year.
VISUAL: the bulb goes out; 452 tiny dots fill the frame, each lighting up; the "one big thing" is one big block that cracks.

BEAT 5  THE PRESTIGE (5:30-6:30)  [the cold-open page returns; the tick stops]
VO: So here is the trick. The moat was never in the number. A company running this loop can show flat margins for years, because it chose to. The saving did not disappear. It went to you, and it came back as size. [OUR NOTE - not Sleep's]
VO: It is why he ran his fund the way he did. Few investments, held for long periods.
QUOTE: "Zak and I don't want to be busy; we want to be right."  (p.151)
VO: And why, years later, he wrote that doing nothing is still a decision.
QUOTE: "the decision not to do something is still an active decision; it is just that the accountants don't capture it."  (p.207)
VO: Since the fund began in September 2001, the letter prints plus one hundred and one point one percent, against plus seven point seven for the world index. Unaudited. Before fees. As printed.
VO: Now go back to your own screen. Before you read a shrinking margin as weakness, ask two questions. Did the company choose it? And where did the savings go?
