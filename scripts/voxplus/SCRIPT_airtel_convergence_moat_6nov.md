# Airtel's Moat: Price, Mix or Convergence?

**Moat & Margin | Visual documentary script | DRAFT for AK's approval | 6 November 2026**

## Production brief — not spoken

Same method as the Jio film: begin with a familiar object (the monthly bill), investigate a specific contradiction, build the explanation through annotated evidence, and return to the opening image with a changed understanding. Moat & Margin branding and original graphics only. Real report pages, cropped and highlighted, for every document shot (never a fabricated page).

**Central question:** How much of Airtel's moat is convergence and postpaid mix (switching costs), and how much is plain pricing power?

**Story:** One bill → three suspects (price, postpaid mix, convergence) → what each can be pinned down to → whether stickiness shows in the money → scale and share → what the balance sheet buys → the answer.

**What the data shows (AK's frame, from the research):** ARPU is lifting mobile growth (about three-fifths) but Airtel does not split tariff from mix; postpaid is only 8.3% of customers; the convergence base is large (15.4 million homes) but Homes ARPU has fallen for eight quarters and its EBIT margin is 6.7% against 16.2%; mobile churn is 2.9%, the same as a year ago; the 'nearly 50% lower churn' is management's claim with no base and is quantified nowhere else; TRAI's headline share flatters Airtel by its machine-to-machine connections.

**Format:** SAY is the complete voice-over. PICTURE specifies the visual sequence. SCREEN gives display copy. EDIT gives pacing and sound directions. Only SAY goes into the voice generator. No spoken chapter numbers.

**Source status:** Every spoken figure is in `airtel_ledger.csv` (one row per figure, same columns as `jioscale.csv`) with the claim id and the document page. Research claims were checked by independent readers (verify_A.md, verify_B.md in this folder). The 'checked_by' and 'checked_on' ledger columns are left blank for AK to sign.

**Timing:** About 1207 spoken words (the Jio film carried 1,296 for 10:30). Scene times are provisional edit targets, not measured audio. Retime after voice generation. Let charts finish and give viewers time to read their conclusions.

**Treatment rules:** No IPO details, price band, valuation, share price or share-price chart, no buy/sell/hold, no moat rating ('narrow/wide' is not used). No Airtel Black churn, customer-lifetime multiples or consensus numbers (not in any filing). Management statements are always labelled 'management says'; our arithmetic is always labelled. The AI voice is disclosed in the description (Altered or synthetic content: Yes).

## 02 | 0:12–1:05 | Name the question

**SAY:**

That number is called ARPU: average revenue per user, per month. Airtel reports it for its Indian mobile business, from three hundred and seventy seven million customers.

A telecom moat can come from three places. Scale: a vast network shared by many customers. Switching costs: it hurts to leave. And pricing power: you can charge more, and customers stay.

Airtel's pitch is the second one. It says bundling mobile, broadband and TV, what it calls convergence, makes customers stickier. So we tested that against the filings.

Not against opinions. Against Airtel's own results, its rivals' filings, and the regulator's data. Where the data cannot answer, we will say so.

**PICTURE:**

Zoom out from the bill to a labelled definition card: ARPU = mobile revenue ÷ average customers ÷ months (Airtel's definition, shown from its Quarterly Report).
Three icons draw in: network (scale), a door with a lock (switching costs), a price tag (pricing power).
The three suspect cards from scene 01 move to the centre and stay.

**SCREEN:**

## 03 | 1:05–2:15 | The line itself

**SAY:**

Start with the line. In September twenty twenty four, ARPU was two hundred and thirty three rupees. Today, two hundred and seventy one. Sixteen percent more, over eight quarters of change.

It did not rise in a straight line. It stayed at two hundred and forty five for two quarters, and slipped in the March twenty twenty six quarter before climbing again.

Now split the growth. Mobile revenue is up ten percent on last year. The customer base is up three point six percent. ARPU is up five point nine.

So, very roughly, three-fifths of the extra revenue comes from each customer paying more, and two-fifths from having more customers. That is our simplification, not Airtel's. And customer additions are slowing: seven hundred and sixteen thousand this quarter, against three point three million in June.

One more caution. Management says both last quarter's two hundred and sixty four and this quarter's two hundred and seventy one had the benefit of an extra day. So the quarter-to-quarter climb is less steep than it looks.

## 04 | 2:15–3:20 | Suspect one: price

**SAY:**

Suspect one: price. Airtel's filings show no dated tariff increase. But in Airtel's own recording of this quarter's call, management says it took a pricing intervention, and that it has played out. It also says a move like that always has a temporary effect on customers staying.

But ARPU was rising before this move. In its earlier calls, management said it was growing ARPU in the absence of any tariff repair, through upgrades, data and roaming. And it does not say how much of the lift comes from the new move.

So price is a real suspect, but not the only one. The filings cannot say how much it explains.

**PICTURE:**

Then two text quote cards, each labelled MANAGEMENT SAYS and 'our transcription of the company's official audio' (Airtel's own recording of the Q2 FY27 call, 6 Nov 2026; never played): 'the intervention that we have taken on pricing... has already kind of played out' (00:22:16) and 'the pricing intervention that we took, where there is always a temporary impact on customer continuity' (00:44:25).

## 05 | 3:20–4:25 | Suspect two: postpaid

Suspect two: postpaid. Airtel added one point two million postpaid customers this quarter, which it calls its best ever, taking the base to thirty one point two million.

That sounds large. But postpaid is still only eight point three percent of its mobile customers. A year ago, seven point six.

Airtel does not disclose postpaid ARPU, so we cannot say how much more a postpaid customer pays, or how much of its ARPU comes from them. So mix may be helping, and by how much is not something the filings say.

## 06 | 4:25–5:25 | Suspect three: convergence

**SAY:**

Suspect three: convergence. This is the big bet. Airtel's home broadband and fibre business now has fifteen point four million customers, three point four million more than a year ago. It is adding homes more slowly, though: six hundred and seventy six thousand this quarter, against nine hundred and fifty one thousand a year ago.

Management says that when a customer has both Wi-Fi and mobile, their churn, the rate at which they leave, drops by nearly fifty percent. It gave no base for that number. In the next quarter's call, management said convergence will be an important lever.

So the question is whether those fifteen million homes are paying for the bet. Let us look at what they earn.

## 07 | 5:25–6:45 | What the homes earn

**SAY:**

Homes revenue this quarter: two thousand three hundred and thirty six crore rupees. Profit before interest and tax: one hundred and fifty seven crore. A margin of six point seven percent. A year ago, sixteen point two.

Revenue per home has fallen every quarter for two years. From five hundred and sixty six rupees to five hundred and nine. Ten percent lower, while in the last year alone the number of homes grew by more than a quarter. On this quarter's call, management says the latest fall is a one-off, a carry-over from low-priced sales it corrected last quarter.

And the business spent two thousand one hundred and eighty one crore on capex in the quarter. Its EBITDA was one thousand one hundred and forty crore. So, after capex, about one thousand and forty one crore more went out than came in.

Airtel itself says its share of homes is, in the long term, a very significant moat. Maybe. But so far, the numbers we can see show an investment, not yet a return.

## 08 | 6:45–7:40 | Does stickiness show?

**SAY:**

If customers were stickier, churn should be falling. Airtel's monthly mobile churn is two point nine percent. A year ago, also two point nine. Last quarter, two point six. Management says this quarter's churn reflects seasonality and the pricing move, and that the pricing effect is temporary. We cannot test that yet.

Management also says the fast growth in fixed-wireless homes brought higher churn in some customer groups, and that it tightened the quality of its sales.

Digital TV, the third leg of the bundle, lost four hundred and forty three thousand customers this quarter, at a loss of about one hundred and thirty two crore rupees. Its monthly churn is three point five percent.

## 09 | 7:40–8:35 | Scale and share

**SAY:**

What about scale? The regulator's September data puts Jio at thirty nine point three percent of mobile connections, and Airtel at thirty eight. Airtel edged up from June.

But Airtel's count includes eighty nine million machine-to-machine connections, sixty one percent of all of them in India. Take those out, and Airtel has about thirty five percent of ordinary mobile connections. Jio, about forty two.

Measured by active share, the share of connections actually in use, the two are level: about ninety nine percent each.

So scale is real. The headline flatters Airtel a little.

**PICTURE:**

## 10 | 8:35–9:25 | What the balance sheet buys

**SAY:**

The money side gives Airtel time. For the whole group, including Africa, net debt, including lease obligations, is about one point two times a year's EBITDA, down from about one point six a year ago. Return on capital is twenty three percent, up from nineteen.

It is also investing heavily. Capex was twelve thousand four hundred and sixty seven crore rupees, plus two thousand eight hundred and twenty six crore for land for data centres, which Airtel reports separately. Net finance costs rose to five thousand and fifty eight crore, from four thousand four hundred and twenty eight.

Net income before one-off items rose thirty three percent, to nine thousand and fifteen crore rupees. We leave out a comparison with Jio's margin. The two companies measure it on different bases.

## 11 | 9:25–10:15 | Return to the bill

**SAY:**

So, which suspect is it? Our arithmetic says ARPU explains about three-fifths of the growth in mobile revenue, but Airtel does not say how much of that is price. Postpaid is real, and small. Convergence is large in homes, not yet paying in the money, and its main promised benefit, lower churn, is a claim without a base.

Our view, as analysis and not a filing fact: today, Airtel's moat shows more in pricing and scale than in proven stickiness. Another reading is also fair: convergence may be an investment whose payoff comes later.

Four things will settle it. Churn. Revenue and margin per home. The next tariff move. And Airtel's own filed transcript of this quarter's call, to check the wording.

Two hundred and seventy one rupees a month. Convergence is meant to protect it. The filings show ARPU lifting it. They do not say how much is price.
