# Jio’s Size Advantage Has a Catch

**Moat & Margin | Visual documentary script | Revised 20 October 2026**

## Production brief — not spoken

A Vox-inspired visual explainer: begin with a familiar object, investigate a specific contradiction, build the explanation through annotated evidence, and return to the opening image with a changed understanding. Use Moat & Margin branding and original graphic treatments.

**Central question:** How much of Jio’s enormous scale turns into a durable financial advantage?

**Story:** One recharge → two business models → the network underneath → customer behaviour → investment and obligations → the answer.

**Revision 20 Oct 2026 (AK, reviewer input):** scene 01 narration replaced with the concrete ₹100 → ~₹20.5 opening; visuals: recharge → ₹100 revenue → ~₹20.5 net profit → CASH LEFT?, labelled Jio Platforms consolidated (not one recharge). Look: original VoxPlus.

**Format:** SAY is the complete voice-over. PICTURE specifies the visual sequence. SCREEN gives display copy. EDIT gives pacing and sound directions. Only SAY goes into the voice generator. No spoken chapter numbers or five-wall scorecard.

**Source status:** Editorial adaptation of the supplied research. Figures have not been independently authenticated in this revision. Use actual source documents for document shots and verify retained calculations before recording. The original references are retained in the production handoff below. Never fabricate a report screenshot.

**Timing:** The opening is designed for ten seconds. Scene times are provisional edit targets, not measured audio durations. Retiming after voice generation is required. Let charts finish and give viewers time to read their conclusions.

**IPO treatment:** No dates, price band, issue size, ownership or application instructions in the narration. Optional two-second pointer at the end only.

## 02 | 0:10–0:55 | Establish the contradiction

**SAY:**

Jio built India's largest subscriber base. That should give it an advantage: more customers sharing the cost of a vast network.

But size answers only one question—how many people use the service.

It doesn't tell us how much each customer earns the company. Or how much investment is needed to keep them connected.

To see the difference, we need to follow this payment through the business. Then compare the result with Airtel.

Because a network can be enormous, while the financial advantage it creates is much smaller than you expect.

## 03 | 0:55–1:55 | The comparison that starts the investigation

**SAY:**

Here are two estimates from the financial-year twenty-six figures.

Jio's telecom business: around one hundred and nineteen rupees of EBITDA per customer, per month. Airtel's India mobile business: around one hundred and fifty-four.

The difference is about thirty-five rupees.

EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. It measures one stage of earnings, before several important costs.

There's also a catch in this chart. These businesses cover different activities, so the figures aren't perfectly comparable.

Still, they challenge a simple assumption: the company with more customers must have better economics per customer.

Airtel emphasises higher-value customers. Jio has built extraordinary scale.

To understand what that scale delivers, first watch what happens to one hundred rupees of Jio's revenue.

## 04 | 1:55–2:55 | A payment becomes an accounting picture

**SAY:**

These hundred rupees represent revenue across Jio Platforms. This isn't a literal breakdown of your individual recharge.

About twenty-six go towards running the network, including rent in our calculation. Eight go towards licence and spectrum charges. Staff, selling and other operating costs take another sixteen.

Around fifty remain, before depreciation, finance costs and tax, excluding other income.

Now account for the assets wearing out or being used over time, financing costs, tax and other income.

Net profit comes to about twenty and a half rupees.

But the timing of cash is different. Equipment can be bought today and depreciated over years. Spectrum can be acquired before all its instalments are paid.

Keep that distinction in mind. First, let's look at the network this money supports.

**PICTURE:**

Use a waterfall or 100-unit block chart. Animate each operating expense group out of the original total. Label the halfway point accurately: “EBITDA excluding other income”.

Then show the second reconciliation: −₹18.6 depreciation/amortisation, −₹5.9 finance costs, −₹7.0 tax, +₹2.0 other income, leaving approximately ₹20.5 net profit. These details appear as graphics; the narration need not read every number.

At the final paragraph, separate the accounting chart from a calendar labelled “Cash payments”. Follow a network expenditure line into a real tower shot.

## 05 | 2:55–3:50 | Show how scale could work

**SAY:**

A tower needs a site, equipment, power and a connection to the rest of the network.

Some of those costs exist before the next customer uses a single gigabyte. Adding customers and traffic can spread the costs more widely—until capacity needs expanding again.

That's the economic promise of Jio's scale.

But measuring the advantage is difficult. Jio's reported traffic includes home broadband. The Airtel traffic comparison here is mobile only.

Divide costs by those different traffic totals, and you risk comparing different kinds of networks.

Scale gives Jio a plausible cost advantage. The available comparison doesn't tell us its precise size.

And capacity depends on another resource, one you can't see from the street: spectrum.

**PICTURE:**

Annotate real tower footage with site, power, equipment and backhaul. Transition to a schematic: one tower serves more phones; then additional capacity appears. Explicitly label the animation “Illustration”.

Show the two traffic definitions from actual reports. Place a house icon beside Jio's phone icon and a phone icon beside Airtel. Visually stop a proposed division calculation when the scope difference appears.

Zoom from the tower antenna into the spectrum graphic for the next scene.

**SCREEN:**

MORE TRAFFIC CAN SPREAD COSTS

Comparison limit: mobile + home broadband vs mobile

**EDIT:**

Use a purposeful visual change at the capacity expansion. Avoid the previous bus analogy; explain the actual system on screen.

## 06 | 3:50–4:55 | Unpack the spectrum headline

**SAY:**

Spectrum is the range of radio frequencies a network uses to carry signals.

But different bands do different jobs. Higher frequencies can provide substantial capacity over shorter distances. Lower frequencies generally travel farther and penetrate buildings better.

Now look inside Jio's spectrum total.

About eighty-two percent of the aggregated bandwidth in these figures is in the twenty-six-gigahertz band. Airtel owns substantial bandwidth there too.

That band is useful. Its large contribution also explains why a single total cannot describe every aspect of network strength.

One distinctive Jio asset is its nationwide seven-hundred-megahertz holding, supporting wider reach and indoor coverage.

The advantage depends on the mix, geography and deployment—not just the largest number.

But even a well-equipped network faces a very ordinary question: will the customer keep paying?

**PICTURE:**

Reveal a simple frequency strip. Keep “Frequency of the band” distinct from “Bandwidth held”; do not mix GHz and MHz as equivalent quantities.

Show the actual holdings table. Extract the supplied 26,800.8 MHz aggregate into a stacked bar, highlighting 22,000 MHz associated with the 26 GHz band. Label the aggregation across licence areas.

Show 700 MHz coverage characteristics using a schematic building cross-section. Do not present the drawing as measured service coverage.

**SCREEN:**

TOTAL BANDWIDTH ≠ COMPLETE NETWORK ADVANTAGE

26 GHz: capacity | 700 MHz: reach

**EDIT:**

Animate the 82% partition, then pause. Keep the distinction between a band's centre frequency and bandwidth in the graphic labels. No implied equivalence between all units of spectrum.

## 07 | 4:55–5:50 | The customers who leave

**SAY:**

These dots represent one thousand connections.

Applying the reported monthly churn rates, Jio's rate corresponds to roughly seventeen disconnections. Airtel's, roughly twenty-four.

This is an illustration of a monthly rate. It doesn't track the same thousand people for a year.

In the three years examined, Jio reported lower churn. That is an encouraging retention signal.

But the dots don't tell us why people stayed. It might be price, coverage, bundles, convenience—or a combination.

So retention gives us evidence of customer behaviour. It doesn't automatically prove customers are locked in.

There's a useful way to test the strength of that relationship: watch what happens when the price changes.

**PICTURE:**

## 08 | 5:50–6:45 | What a price rise reveals

**SAY:**

When tariffs rose, Jio's average revenue per user increased. But churn also rose, and subscriber growth slowed before recovering.

That's why a higher revenue-per-user figure needs a second chart beside it.

One shows what customers pay. The other shows how customers respond.

A company has stronger pricing power when it can keep more of the benefit after departures, costs and competitive responses.

For Jio, the evidence here is mixed. Higher revenue is encouraging. The customer response still matters.

And it takes more than Jio's own network to reach those customers. Follow the distribution and infrastructure relationships, and another part of the business comes into view.

## 09 | 6:45–7:35 | The relationships underneath the network

**SAY:**

Reliance Retail distributes Jio's prepaid connectivity. Large parts of the tower and fibre network involve other companies under long-term arrangements.

Those relationships can help a network expand efficiently. They also make contract terms important.

Who sets the price? What service is promised? What happens when an agreement changes or expires?

We don't have enough detail here to judge every arrangement. The point is that reach depends partly on relationships, not only on assets inside one company.

Now step back. Jio has scale, useful spectrum and encouraging retention. There is a serious case that these advantages could produce better results as the investment cycle changes.

## 10 | 7:35–8:30 | The strongest positive case

**SAY:**

This chart shows cash capital expenditure as a share of revenue: roughly forty-nine percent, falling to twenty-three percent over two years.

A network requires substantial investment before it earns its full potential. If an existing network can support more paying customers without equally large new spending, cash generation can improve.

That's the strongest positive case for Jio: scale and retention, followed by a less demanding phase of investment.

But lower spending in one period doesn't mean investment is finished. And it doesn't mean every earlier bill has been paid.

Remember the cash calendar we set aside? Now we need it back.

Because one definition changes how we read the debt number.

## 11 | 8:30–9:30 | The footnote, explained

**SAY:**

Here, net debt is shown at zero point three six times EBITDA.

Now look at the definition. It excludes deferred spectrum payment liabilities: money still owed for spectrum acquired earlier.

Include those obligations in our calculation, and the ratio becomes about one point seven times.

The business hasn't changed between these two numbers. The definition has.

This obligation is disclosed. The important step is to understand what each measure includes, and when the payments fall due.

That's why the cash calendar matters. Subscriber growth, reported profit and a debt ratio each show part of the picture.

To understand the whole business, we need to connect them: what comes in, what must be reinvested, and what is still owed.

**PICTURE:**

Show the real page containing the 0.36× metric. Crop into the actual exclusion note; highlight only the relevant words.

Place the same EBITDA denominator beneath two numerator boxes: reported net debt, then reported net debt plus deferred spectrum liabilities. Animate the added category before changing the resulting ratio to ~1.73×.

Keep the full deferred liability amount, ₹1,04,514 crore, in the reconciliation, but narration need not read it. Do not invent a repayment timetable if the underlying schedule is unavailable.

**SCREEN:**

## 12 | 9:30–10:30 | Return to the recharge

**SAY:**

So let's return to this recharge.

It helps support a business with enormous scale, distinctive spectrum assets and encouraging retention. Those are real reasons to take Jio seriously.

But scale becomes a durable financial advantage only when it produces better economics after the costs of maintaining, expanding and financing the network.

Our assessment is a narrow moat: some protection, with important questions about pricing power, comparable costs and returns on the capital invested.

The next evidence to watch is straightforward. Can Jio earn more per customer, keep its retention advantage, and turn that combination into stronger cash generation and capital returns?

That's how we'll know whether its size advantage is getting stronger.

Your recharge ends when the payment clears. For Jio, that's where the economics begin.

## Visual system and asset brief — not spoken

- **Frame:** 16:9. Build for phone readability even in a landscape video. Keep source labels visible and accessible subtitles separate from main graphic text.
- **Brand:** Moat & Margin identity. Suggested palette: warm off-white, charcoal, muted teal and rust. Label company series directly; colour alone must not carry meaning. Do not copy Vox logos, typography packages or signature branding.
- **Typography:** One readable sans serif with two weights. Large claim or number, smaller explanation, consistent source footer. Avoid paragraphs on screen.
- **Document shots:** Real report pages, then a crop, then a highlight. Retain document title, reporting date and page reference. If the document is not available, leave an editor placeholder; never create simulated evidence.
- **Charts:** Direct labels, zero-based bars, explicit units, periods and entity scopes. No invented intermediate points. Tables in the source handoff are production instructions, not graphics to flash on screen.
- **Transitions:** Match the shape or object between scenes: recharge → rupee units → tower → spectrum → connection dots → recharge price → distribution map → capex → debt definition → original recharge.
- **Pacing:** Introduce a visual when the explanation needs it. A report highlight may need 5–8 seconds; a complex chart may need longer. Avoid cutting merely to reach a fixed cut frequency.
- **Sound:** Restrained music under narration; purposeful payment click and soft graphic cues. Brief quiet at the debt definition. Avoid constant whooshes.
- **Stock footage:** Use only where it establishes the real system: phone use, tower equipment, fibre work. It should not replace the explanatory graphics.
- **Recurring device:** The small payment marker and cash calendar connect the film. Bus, restaurant, landlord and hotel analogies from the prior version have been replaced by visuals of the actual telecom system.
